A3 — Gas exchange and circulation

Follow oxygen all the way to a working cell

During increased activity, muscle ATP demand rises. Breathing faster is only one possible response. Oxygen must reach the respiratory surface, cross a barrier, enter an internal transport system where needed, leave that system near the tissue and reach mitochondria. This unit follows that pathway and connects ventilation, exchange surfaces, blood and the pump.

By the end, explain diffusion and bulk flow as different processes; compare fish, mammalian and insect gas transport; interpret oxygen content separately from partial pressure; trace mammalian circulation; and connect vessel and respiratory histology to function. Work through the slides, exercises and quiz.

Diffusion needs a gradient and a suitable barrier

A gas diffuses according to differences in its chemical potential, commonly described in physiology using partial pressure. Partial pressure is the pressure contribution of one gas in a mixture. A gas in solution also has an associated partial pressure related to dissolved, unbound gas. Oxygen can have the same partial pressure in two fluids while their total oxygen contents differ greatly because one contains an oxygen-binding pigment.

For a simple barrier, transfer rate increases with area and the partial-pressure difference and decreases with barrier thickness. We can write the proportional relationship as \(\dot M \propto A\Delta P/L\), holding the material’s diffusion properties constant. Doubling area doubles predicted transfer; doubling thickness halves it. This is a simplified comparison, because actual respiratory surfaces also have flow, varying gradients and heterogeneous structure.

Thinness and large area bring costs. A delicate moist surface needs protection, and a surface exposed to air can lose water. A respiratory organ must therefore combine exchange efficiency with support, protection and ventilation. In mammalian lung tissue, thin alveolar walls and nearby capillaries shorten the route between air and blood. Larger airways have a different task: conducting air, with supporting and protective structures rather than gas exchange across their full walls. See the openly accessible overview of exchange systems.

Ventilation and perfusion renew the two sides

Ventilation moves environmental medium over a respiratory surface. Perfusion supplies internal fluid, often blood, to that surface. Both can maintain gradients by replacing fluid that has already exchanged gases. Increasing one alone may do little if the other is severely limiting.

A fish gill presents a large thin area to water. Water and blood commonly move in opposite directions across exchange regions. In an ideal countercurrent arrangement, a gradient can persist along the exchange path, allowing more complete extraction than a comparable co-current arrangement. It is not necessary for oxygen to move “uphill”: at every exchange location the net diffusion remains down a local partial-pressure gradient. Maintaining flow and supporting the gill are themselves physiological tasks.

Mammalian lungs use tidal ventilation: air moves in and out along shared conducting pathways. Fresh incoming air mixes with air already present, and not every part of an inspired breath reaches exchange regions. Birds provide a useful contrast: their rigid exchange lungs and air-sac ventilation support a different flow arrangement. Avoid describing air sacs themselves as the main gas-exchanging surface. The details of avian flow exceed this first explanation; the key point is that lungs are not all built as mammalian alveolar sacs.

Insects provide a further contrast. Tracheae and finer tracheoles bring gases close to tissues; ventilation and diffusion contribute to transport. Their circulating haemolymph generally has a smaller role in oxygen transport than vertebrate blood. This is an important counterexample to the claim that every active animal requires a vertebrate-style blood oxygen pathway.

Oxygen pressure, content and delivery

In many vertebrates, haemoglobin carries most blood oxygen. Dissolved oxygen contributes to partial pressure; haemoglobin-bound oxygen greatly increases content. Saturation is the fraction of available binding sites occupied, whereas oxygen content is an amount per volume of blood. Neither is a synonym for partial pressure. A blood sample can have a normal oxygen partial pressure yet carry less total oxygen if it contains less haemoglobin.

Cooperative oxygen binding produces an S-shaped relationship between haemoglobin saturation and oxygen partial pressure under typical conditions. The relation supports loading at high partial pressure and unloading as partial pressure falls in tissues. Temperature, pH and carbon dioxide influence affinity, helping coordinate delivery with local conditions. A rightward shift means lower affinity at a stated partial pressure; it does not mean oxygen has disappeared from the system.

Carbon dioxide returns from tissues in several forms, including bicarbonate, bound compounds and dissolved gas. Conversion between carbon dioxide and bicarbonate connects gas transport with acid–base balance. Ventilation can change carbon dioxide loss, but oxygen and carbon dioxide regulation should not be collapsed into a single “more breathing is always better” rule. The supporting reading distinguishes gas exchange and transport.

For a simplified steady-state accounting, oxygen use equals blood flow multiplied by the arterial–venous oxygen-content difference. If flow is 5 L min⁻¹, arterial content 200 mL O₂ L⁻¹ and venous content 150 mL O₂ L⁻¹, oxygen use is \(5(200-150)=250\) mL O₂ min⁻¹. The product uses content, not oxygen partial pressure. The calculation is a whole-system balance and requires representative measurements under the assumed conditions.

A circuit needs pressure differences and valves

In mammalian circulation, the right heart sends blood towards the lungs and the left heart sends blood towards systemic tissues. Trace the circuit: systemic veins → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → systemic arteries. An artery carries blood away from the heart; a vein returns it. Oxygenation does not define these vessel names.

This original circuit sketch shows direction, not anatomical position or the relative size of its compartments.

Valves help prevent reverse flow as chamber pressures change. The ventricular wall develops pressure, but pressure is not identical to flow. A simple relation is flow \(Q=\Delta P/R\), where \(R\) is vascular resistance. With a fixed pressure difference, higher resistance gives lower flow. Smooth muscle can alter vessel calibre and distribute flow among tissues. Narrow vessels have high resistance individually, yet many small vessels arranged in parallel can support substantial total flow and a very large exchange area.

Arteries generally have walls suited to pressure and its fluctuations. Capillaries provide short exchange paths. Veins can hold a substantial blood volume at relatively low pressure, and return is influenced by pressure gradients, surrounding muscle and valves in appropriate locations. An empty or collapsed vessel in a section can look different from a blood-filled one; describe wall layers and surrounding tissue before naming it.

Comparative circuits and coordinated responses

A fish typically sends blood from its heart through gills and then systemic tissues in a single circuit. Mammals separate pulmonary and systemic circuits with a four-chambered heart. Other vertebrates have other arrangements, including variable separation of blood streams. Open circulations move haemolymph through vessels and body spaces; closed circulations keep the main transport fluid within vessels. “Open” does not mean motionless, and “closed” does not automatically identify the animal’s metabolic rate. Compare the actual route, pressure, control and exchange requirements. A useful overview is the reading on circulatory organisation.

At the onset of exercise, ventilation, cardiac activity and local vascular responses can act together. Cardiac output is heart rate times stroke volume. Increasing heart rate is only one way to change output, and a measured rate alone does not establish how much blood or oxygen reaches a tissue. Local metabolites and neural/endocrine signals help coordinate supply and demand.
