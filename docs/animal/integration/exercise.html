A5 exercises — Nerve, muscle, hormones and reproduction

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Membrane potential

1.1

The Nernst equation at 37 °C is

\[E_\text{ion} = \frac{61.5\ \text{mV}}{z}\,\log_{10}\frac{[\text{ion}]_\text{out}}{[\text{ion}]_\text{in}},\]

where \(z\) is the charge of the ion. Calculate the equilibrium potential of each ion in a mammalian neuron.

Ion Outside (mmol L⁻¹) Inside (mmol L⁻¹)
K⁺ 5 140
Na⁺ 145 15
Cl⁻ 110 10
Answer
  • \(E_\text{K} = 61.5 \log_{10}(5/140) = 61.5 \times (-1.45) \approx -89\) mV.
  • \(E_\text{Na} = 61.5 \log_{10}(145/15) = 61.5 \times 0.985 \approx +61\) mV.
  • \(E_\text{Cl} = -61.5 \log_{10}(110/10) = -61.5 \times 1.04 \approx -64\) mV (\(z = -1\)).

1.2

The neuron in 1.1 has a resting membrane potential of −70 mV.

  1. Which ion’s equilibrium potential is the resting potential closest to, and what does this tell you about the resting membrane?
  2. In which direction is the net electrochemical driving force on Na⁺?
  3. In which direction is the net electrochemical driving force on K⁺?
Answer
  1. K⁺. At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ (through leak channels) than to Na⁺.
  2. Inward: both the concentration gradient and the negative inside favour Na⁺ entry.
  3. Outward. The membrane potential (−70 mV) is less negative than \(E_\text{K}\) (−89 mV), so the concentration gradient outweighs the electrical attraction.

1.3

Extracellular K⁺ is raised from 5 to 10 mmol L⁻¹ (intracellular K⁺ stays at 140 mmol L⁻¹). Calculate the new \(E_\text{K}\). Does the membrane depolarise or hyperpolarise?

Answer

\(E_\text{K} = 61.5 \log_{10}(10/140) \approx -70.5\) mV. \(E_\text{K}\) becomes less negative, so the resting membrane depolarises.

1.4

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ into the cell per ATP.
  2. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase directly produces the rising phase of the action potential.
  3. The inside of a resting neuron is negative because all the molecules in the cytoplasm are negatively charged.
  4. During a single action potential, the intracellular Na⁺ concentration changes only very slightly.
Answer
  1. True.
  2. False. The rising phase is caused by Na⁺ flowing in through voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, down its electrochemical gradient. The pump maintains the gradients over the longer term.
  3. False. The cytoplasm as a whole is electrically neutral; only a very small excess of negative charge lies next to the membrane.
  4. True. Very few ions need to cross the membrane to change the voltage.

The action potential

2.1

Put the events of an axonal action potential in order.

  1. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open and K⁺ leaves the cell.
  2. The membrane is depolarised to threshold.
  3. The membrane briefly hyperpolarises below the resting potential.
  4. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ enters the cell.
  5. Na⁺ channels inactivate.
  6. The membrane potential rises rapidly and becomes positive (overshoot).
  7. K⁺ channels close and the resting potential is restored.
Answer

b → d → f → e → a → c → g. Na⁺-channel inactivation and K⁺-channel opening together cause repolarisation.

2.2

Define the absolute and the relative refractory period, and give the cause of each.

Answer
  • Absolute refractory period: no stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second action potential. Cause: the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are inactivated.
  • Relative refractory period: a stronger-than-normal stimulus is needed to trigger an action potential. Cause: some Na⁺ channels are still inactivated and K⁺ conductance is still raised (the membrane is hyperpolarised).

The refractory period makes the action potential travel in one direction only.

2.3

A stronger stimulus is applied to a sensory receptor. Which two properties of the sensory signal increase? Which property stays the same?

Answer

The frequency of action potentials in each neuron and the number of neurons recruited both increase. The amplitude of each action potential stays the same, because the action potential is all-or-none.

2.4

  1. Name two properties of an axon that increase its conduction velocity.
  2. An action potential travels 0.60 m from the spinal cord to a foot muscle in 12 ms. Calculate the conduction velocity in m s⁻¹.
Answer
  1. A larger axon diameter, and myelination (saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier).
  2. 0.60 m ÷ 0.012 s = 50 m s⁻¹.

Synapses and reflexes

3.1

Put the events at a chemical synapse in order.

  1. Transmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open in the presynaptic terminal.
  3. An action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal.
  4. Synaptic vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release transmitter by exocytosis.
  5. Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.
  6. Ion channels open and the postsynaptic membrane potential changes.
  7. Transmitter is removed by enzymatic breakdown or reuptake.
Answer

c → b → e → d → a → f → g.

3.2

State whether each postsynaptic response is excitatory or inhibitory.

  1. Acetylcholine opens cation channels (nicotinic receptors) on a skeletal muscle fibre.
  2. Acetylcholine binds muscarinic receptors on cardiac pacemaker cells and opens K⁺ channels.
  3. GABA opens Cl⁻ channels, and the membrane potential moves towards \(E_\text{Cl}\) (−70 mV) from a threshold of −55 mV.
  4. Glutamate opens channels permeable to Na⁺ and K⁺.
Answer
  1. Excitatory. b. Inhibitory (it slows the heart). c. Inhibitory.
  2. Excitatory.

Examples a and b show that the same transmitter can excite or inhibit: the effect depends on the receptor.

3.3

Name each component of the knee-jerk (patellar) reflex.

Component Structure in the knee-jerk reflex
Receptor
Afferent pathway
Integrating centre
Efferent pathway
Effector
Answer
Component Structure in the knee-jerk reflex
Receptor Muscle spindle (stretch receptor) in the quadriceps
Afferent pathway Sensory neuron
Integrating centre Spinal cord (one synapse: a monosynaptic reflex)
Efferent pathway Motor neuron
Effector Quadriceps muscle

The reflex does not need the brain, so it is faster than a voluntary response.

Skeletal muscle

4.1

Put the steps of excitation–contraction coupling in skeletal muscle in order.

  1. Ca²⁺ binds to troponin.
  2. Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction.
  3. Ca²⁺ is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  4. An action potential arrives at the motor-neuron terminal.
  5. Tropomyosin moves and exposes the myosin-binding sites on actin.
  6. The muscle action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and into the T tubules.
  7. Myosin heads bind actin and perform power strokes.
  8. An end-plate potential depolarises the muscle fibre to threshold.
  9. Voltage-sensing (dihydropyridine) receptors in the T-tubule membrane open the ryanodine receptors of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
Answer

d → b → h → f → i → c → a → e → g.

Relaxation follows when acetylcholine is broken down by acetylcholinesterase and Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by an ATP-driven Ca²⁺ pump.

4.2

Give the role of each step in the cross-bridge cycle.

  1. ATP binding to myosin
  2. ATP hydrolysis by myosin
  3. Release of phosphate from myosin

Why do muscles become rigid after death (rigor mortis)?

Answer
  1. Detaches the myosin head from actin.
  2. Re-cocks the myosin head into its high-energy position.
  3. Triggers the power stroke, which pulls the thin filament towards the centre of the sarcomere.

After death ATP runs out, so myosin heads stay bound to actin and cannot detach.

4.3

When a sarcomere shortens, which of the following become shorter and which stay the same length?

  1. A band
  2. I band
  3. H zone
  4. Distance between Z discs
  5. Thick (myosin) filaments
  6. Thin (actin) filaments
Answer

Shorter: b, c and d. Unchanged: a, e and f. The filaments slide past each other without changing length (sliding-filament model).

4.4

A muscle lifts a load with a constant force of 20 N through 0.10 m in 0.50 s, with force and displacement in the same direction. It then holds the load still for 5 s.

  1. Calculate the external mechanical work done while lifting.
  2. Calculate the mean external mechanical power while lifting.
  3. Calculate the external work done while holding the load still.
  4. True or false: the muscle uses no ATP while holding the load still.
Answer
  1. \(W = Fd = 20 \times 0.10 = 2.0\) J.
  2. \(P = W/t = 2.0 / 0.50 = 4.0\) W.
  3. 0 J, because there is no displacement.
  4. False. Cross-bridges keep cycling to maintain force (isometric contraction), and Ca²⁺ pumping continues, so ATP is consumed.

4.5

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. A motor unit is one motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it innervates.
  2. Whole-muscle force can be increased by recruiting more motor units.
  3. Whole-muscle force can be increased by raising the frequency of action potentials, so that twitches summate into tetanus.
  4. Whole-muscle force is increased by making each motor-neuron action potential larger.
Answer
  1. True. b. True. c. True.
  2. False. Action potentials are all-or-none; force is graded by recruitment and firing frequency.

4.6

In smooth muscle, Ca²⁺ also triggers contraction, but not through troponin. Name the Ca²⁺-binding protein and the enzyme it activates, and state what the enzyme does.

Answer

Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin. Ca²⁺–calmodulin activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK), which phosphorylates the myosin light chains. This allows myosin to bind actin and cycle.

Complete the table comparing two classes of hormone.

Peptide hormones Steroid hormones
Solubility
Transport in blood
Location of receptor
Main mechanism of action
Two examples
Answer
Peptide hormones Steroid hormones
Solubility Water-soluble Lipid-soluble

Explore: Ion gradients and membrane potential

The left panel shows K⁺ (green) and Na⁺ (red) on both sides of the membrane, with the channel widths showing the relative permeability; the right panel places \(E_\text{K}\), \(E_\text{Na}\) (Nernst) and the membrane potential \(V_m\) (Goldman equation) on one voltage scale. Use the sliders or the preset buttons, and read the values from the readout below the buttons.

E1.1

Click Rest. Read off \(E_\text{K}\), \(E_\text{Na}\) and \(V_m\). Is \(V_m\) closer to \(E_\text{K}\) or to \(E_\text{Na}\)?

Answer

\(E_\text{K}\) = −89 mV, \(E_\text{Na}\) = +61 mV, \(V_m\) = −69 mV (the same values as in 1.1). \(V_m\) is much closer to \(E_\text{K}\), because at rest \(P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K}\) is only 0.04.

E1.2

Click K⁺ channels only (\(P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K} \approx 0\)). What is \(V_m\) now, and what is the K⁺ driving force?

Answer

\(V_m\) = −88 mV, practically equal to \(E_\text{K}\) (−89 mV); the K⁺ driving force is only +1 mV. A membrane permeable to one ion only sits at that ion’s Nernst potential.

E1.3

Click Rest, then drag K⁺ outside from 5 to 10. Read off \(E_\text{K}\) and \(V_m\). Then click K⁺ out = 20 and read them again. Does raising extracellular K⁺ depolarise or hyperpolarise the cell?

Answer
K⁺ outside (mmol L⁻¹) \(E_\text{K}\) \(V_m\)
5 −89 mV −69 mV
10 −70 mV −58 mV
20 −52 mV −45 mV

It depolarises the cell (compare 1.3). This is why hyperkalaemia is dangerous: excitable cells, including heart muscle, are pushed towards and past threshold.

E1.4

Click Na⁺ channels open (\(P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K}\) = 20). What is \(V_m\)? Did the number of ion dots on either side change noticeably?

Answer

\(V_m\) = +50 mV, close to \(E_\text{Na}\) (+61 mV), as at the peak of an action potential. The dots do not change: the potential reverses through a change in permeability, not in concentration; very few ions need to cross.

E1.5

Click Rest, then set \(P_\text{Na}/P_\text{K}\) to 1 (equal permeabilities). Which value is closest to \(V_m\): −89, −69, −1 or +61 mV?

Answer

−1 mV. With equal permeabilities \(V_m\) lies roughly midway between \(E_\text{K}\) and \(E_\text{Na}\): the membrane potential is a permeability-weighted compromise between the equilibrium potentials.

Explore: Threshold and refractory periods

A Hodgkin–Huxley axon receives two brief current pulses, S1 and S2 (each 0.5 ms). Set their strengths and the S1 → S2 interval with the sliders or the preset buttons. The lower panel shows the fraction of Na⁺ channels that are not inactivated (green) and the open K⁺ channels (gold); the verdict and readout below the buttons report the spikes.

E2.1

Click Too weak (S1 = 12, no S2), then raise S1 strength one step at a time. What is the smallest S1 that produces a spike?

Answer
  1. At 15 the membrane depolarises a little and returns to rest; at 16 a full spike follows. This is the threshold.

E2.2

Click One spike (S1 = 40) and read the peak Vm. Then set S1 to 20 and to 80. What happens to the peak?

  1. It roughly doubles from S1 = 20 to S1 = 80.
  2. It stays about the same (+26 to +32 mV).
  3. The spike disappears at S1 = 80.
Answer
  1. Peak Vm is +30 mV at S1 = 40, +26 mV at 20 and +32 mV at 80. Above threshold the spike is all-or-none: a stronger stimulus does not give a bigger spike (stimulus strength is coded by spike frequency instead). The lowest Vm, the undershoot, is −81 mV in every case.

E2.3

Click 2nd at 1.5 ms, 8× (S2 = 320, eight times S1). Is there a second spike? Which refractory period is this, and what does the green trace show?

Answer

No second spike, even at S2 = 320: the absolute refractory period. The green trace shows that almost all Na⁺ channels are still inactivated, so no stimulus can open enough of them.

E2.4

Click 2nd at 3 ms (S2 = 40). Does S2 fire? According to the verdict, what S2 strength is needed at this interval? Then click 3 ms, 2× stronger: how many spikes now?

Answer

S2 = 40 fails; the verdict says S2 needs about 60 (relative refractory period). At S2 = 80 there are 2 spikes. In the relative refractory period a spike is possible, but only with a stronger stimulus, because some Na⁺ channels are still inactivated and K⁺ channels are still open.

E2.5

Keep S1 = S2 = 40 and drag S1 → S2 upwards from 2 ms in 0.5 ms steps. At which interval does the second spike first appear?

Answer

At 4.0 ms (at 3.5 ms S2 would need about 45). Refractoriness limits the maximum spike frequency and makes spikes travel in one direction only.

Explore: Summation, tetanus and recruitment

The plot shows the force of a muscle of four motor units (sizes 1 to 4, stacked colours) when its motor neurons fire at the chosen rate for 500 ms; red ticks below are the spikes. Set Stimulus rate (0 = one stimulus) and Motor units with the sliders or buttons and read the verdict and readout.

E3.1

With 4 motor units, click 5 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz and 80 Hz in turn. For each, give the verdict and the “peak force / one twitch” value.

Answer
Rate Verdict Peak force / one twitch
5 Hz Separate twitches 1.0×
15 Hz Summation: unfused tetanus 1.6×
30 Hz Fused tetanus 2.4×
80 Hz Fused tetanus 3.4×

At higher rates Ca²⁺ is released again before the previous release has been pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so the twitches summate.

E3.2

Drag the rate slider upwards from 20 Hz. At which rate does the verdict first change to “Fused tetanus”?

Answer

30 Hz. Force keeps rising beyond that (2.4× at 30 Hz, 3.4× at 80 Hz) even though the trace is already smooth.

E3.3

Set the rate to 80 Hz. Read the peak total force with 1, 2, 3 and 4 motor units recruited. Which unit adds the largest step?

Answer

0.9, 2.8, 5.7 and 9.4. The fourth (largest) unit adds the largest step (3.7). Units are recruited smallest first (size principle), so force is graded finely at low force and in large steps at high force.

E3.4

Click Single twitch with 4 units, then 80 Hz with 4 units. Which of the following changed between the two runs?

  1. The size of each motor-neuron spike
  2. The number of motor-neuron spikes
  3. The number of motor units recruited
Answer

Only b (1 stimulus versus 40). Peak total force rose from 2.8 to 9.4 because the muscle summates many twitches; spikes are all-or-none and never got bigger. Muscle force is graded by firing rate and by recruitment (c, not changed here).

Bioinformatics and R

A channel, receptor or hormone is encoded by a gene, and the same gene exists in many species. When two species differ in a trait (a horse that collapses after a potassium-rich meal, a fish that is full of a nerve poison but is not paralysed), comparing the gene between them can show which single amino acid makes the difference. These exercises teach you to find a gene and its orthologues in NCBI Gene and Ensembl, and to use R to fetch the protein sequences, line up the relevant positions and analyse real physiological data. Reading a UniProt FASTA file into R was introduced in A1.

How to search NCBI Gene and Ensembl

NCBI Gene (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene) has one record per gene per species.

  • Use field tags to avoid hundreds of hits: SCN4A[sym] AND human[orgn] means “official gene symbol SCN4A in human”. Latin names go in quotes: "Takifugu rubripes"[orgn]. A * is a wildcard: scn4a*[sym] also finds scn4aa and scn4ab.
  • A plain word such as HYPP AND human[orgn] searches all fields, including aliases and disease names. Several genes may then match; open the one whose official symbol and description fit.
  • Key fields of a record: Gene ID (a stable number, e.g. 6329), Official Symbol, Also known as (aliases, often the protein name such as Nav1.4, and disease abbreviations), Summary, and Genomic context (chromosome, position, exon count).
  • The Orthologs link on a gene page lists the corresponding gene in other species, each with its own Gene ID. RefSeq accessions starting with NM_/NP_ are curated mRNAs/proteins; XM_/XP_ are computer predictions.

Ensembl (https://www.ensembl.org) annotates whole genomes. Type a gene symbol in the search box, choose the species and open the gene. Human gene IDs look like ENSG00000007314 (mouse ENSMUSG…, zebrafish ENSDARG…). The gene page shows location, strand and all transcripts; its orthologue view classifies each orthologue as 1-to-1 or 1-to-many. Many teleost fish have two copies of a mammalian gene (named …a and …b), left over from a genome duplication in their ancestor.

Recap from A3: orthologues are the same gene in different species (split by speciation); paralogues are different genes in the same genome (split by gene duplication).

B1. A horse that collapses after a potassium-rich meal

Some Quarter Horses have attacks of muscle tremor and weakness when their plasma K⁺ rises. The disease is hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis (HYPP), and the same disease occurs in humans. Find the gene responsible.

  1. Search NCBI Gene for HYPP AND human[orgn]. Several genes match. What is the official symbol and the Gene ID of the one that encodes a voltage-gated sodium channel?
  2. Open that record. On which human chromosome does the gene lie?
  3. Which name of the encoded channel, starting with “Nav”, is listed under “Also known as”?
  4. Search Ensembl for the same gene in human. What is its Ensembl gene ID, and is it on the forward or the reverse strand?
  5. Go back to NCBI Gene and open the Orthologs list. What is the Gene ID of the horse (Equus caballus) orthologue, and on which horse chromosome is it?
Answer
  1. SCN4A, Gene ID 6329 (“sodium voltage-gated channel alpha subunit 4”). The other hits mention HYPP for other reasons; only SCN4A is a sodium channel.
  2. Chromosome 17 (17q23.3).
  3. Nav1.4 (the skeletal-muscle sodium channel; SkM1 is an older name). HYPP itself is also in the alias list, which is why the search found it.

B3. How can a pufferfish carry tetrodotoxin without paralysing itself?

Tetrodotoxin (TTX) blocks the outer mouth of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, so action potentials stop. Pufferfish store large amounts of TTX in their bodies. In human Nav1.4, UniProt annotates tyrosine 407 in the domain I pore loop as “important for inhibition by tetrodotoxin”: an aromatic ring there (Y, F or W) binds the toxin.

  1. Search NCBI Gene for scn4a*[sym] AND "Takifugu rubripes"[orgn]. How many Nav1.4 genes does the Japanese pufferfish (fugu) have, what are their symbols, and on which chromosomes are they?

B5. How long does a chemical signal stay in the blood?

A hormone or a drug acts only while its plasma concentration is high enough. Removal by the liver and kidneys usually follows first-order kinetics: a constant fraction is removed per hour, so the concentration falls exponentially and \(\ln(C)\) falls along a straight line with slope \(-k\). The half-life is \(t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k\). The real dataset Theoph (shipped with R) holds plasma concentrations of theophylline, an airway-relaxing drug related to caffeine, in 12 people after one oral dose.

s1 <- Theoph[Theoph$Subject == 1, ]
s1[, c("Time", "conc")]
plot(s1$Time, s1$conc, log = "y", type = "b",
     xlab = "Time after dose (h)", ylab = "Theophylline (mg/L, log scale)")
late <- s1[s1$Time >= 5, ]                 # after absorption is over
fit  <- lm(log(conc) ~ Time, data = late)  # straight line on a log scale
k    <- -coef(fit)[["Time"]]               # elimination rate constant (1/h)
round(k, 3)
round(log(2) / k, 1)                       # half-life (h)

half_life <- function(d) {
  d <- d[d$Time >= 5, ]
  log(2) / -coef(lm(log(conc) ~ Time, data = d))[["Time"]]
}
hl <- sapply(split(Theoph, Theoph$Subject), half_life)
round(sort(hl), 1)
round(median(hl), 1)
round(100 * 0.5^(24 / median(hl)))         # % left after 24 h
