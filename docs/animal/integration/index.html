A5 — Integration, movement and reproduction

How does an animal coordinate a response?

An animal detecting food, escaping a threat or reproducing must connect information to action. Neural activity, endocrine signals and muscle do not operate as isolated topics. They link sensory input and internal state to effectors while using the transport and metabolic support developed in earlier units. This unit builds those links from membrane properties to whole-organism behaviour and reproductive regulation.

You should explain membrane potential and action potentials without confusing voltage with ion concentration; trace a chemical synapse and a reflex pathway; connect calcium, ATP and muscle force; distinguish hormone concentration from target-cell response; and explain a reproductive feedback loop. Use the slides, exercises and quiz.

Ion gradients create possibilities for electrical signals

A membrane potential is a voltage difference across a cell membrane. Ion concentrations differ inside and outside cells, and membranes are selectively permeable. The combined electrical and chemical tendencies determine each ion’s electrochemical driving force. Resting membranes are often more permeable to some ions than others. ATP-dependent transport, including the sodium–potassium pump in many animal cells, maintains gradients that leak pathways would otherwise dissipate.

The inside of a resting neuron is commonly negative relative to the outside. This does not mean that all intracellular material is negatively charged: only a small charge separation near the membrane is needed for a voltage. Opening a channel changes permeability; the direction of ion movement depends on that ion’s electrochemical gradient. A sodium channel is not itself an ATP-driven sodium pump.

A small local electrical change may decay with distance. An action potential is regenerated when voltage-dependent channels activate in adjacent membrane. In a typical vertebrate axon, rapid sodium entry drives depolarisation and subsequent channel changes, including potassium conductance, contribute to repolarisation. Refractory properties limit immediate re-excitation and help organise propagation. Exact waveforms and ion contributions differ among cell types, so treat this as a representative mechanism, not a universal recipe for every excitable cell. The supporting reading explains neuronal communication.

A signal can be coded without becoming a larger spike

Once threshold is reached under comparable conditions, an individual action potential is approximately stereotyped. Stronger stimulation can change firing frequency, timing and the number of recruited neurons rather than making each spike proportionately taller. A sensory neuron is also selective: receptor properties and neural pathways determine what kind of change it can encode. An electrical recording alone does not reveal the animal’s conscious experience.

At a chemical synapse, arrival of an action potential can open presynaptic calcium channels. Calcium entry promotes neurotransmitter release. Transmitter acts on postsynaptic receptors, changing the receiving cell’s activity. Effects depend on receptor type and the target cell’s conditions; the same transmitter name is not a complete explanation of excitation or inhibition.

This original reflex sketch shows a functional route rather than a detailed anatomical circuit. A simple reflex can operate without waiting for conscious decision, while information may also reach other brain regions. The existence of a fast local pathway does not imply that the entire nervous system has one linear input and one output. Integrating excitatory, inhibitory and modulatory signals helps make context-dependent responses possible.

Muscle converts chemical energy into force

Skeletal muscle contains fibres with organised contractile machinery. Within sarcomeres, interactions between actin and myosin generate force through cycles requiring ATP. Calcium exposes regulatory sites in the skeletal-muscle thin filament by acting through troponin and tropomyosin. ATP binding permits myosin to detach; ATP hydrolysis prepares subsequent interactions. Therefore ATP is necessary for controlled cycling and relaxation as well as work.

At a vertebrate neuromuscular junction, motor-neuron signalling triggers a muscle electrical response. That response reaches internal membrane systems and promotes calcium release from intracellular stores. Calcium recovery helps end activation. The sequence connects a neural signal to a mechanical effect: it is more informative than saying “the nerve contracts the muscle”. OpenStax provides a supporting account of muscle contraction.

An isometric contraction develops force without an overall change in muscle length. Mechanical work on a load requires displacement; for a constant force aligned with movement, \(W=Fd\). A muscle can consume ATP while holding a load stationary even though the external mechanical work in that simple calculation is zero. Efficiency calculations must define which energy input and output are being compared.

Force varies with recruitment, activation timing, muscle length and loading conditions. A motor unit consists of a motor neuron and the muscle fibres it innervates. Recruiting more units can increase whole-muscle force without changing the amplitude of each axon’s action potentials. Smooth and cardiac muscle share actin–myosin interactions but differ in organisation and control. Smooth muscle is not simply skeletal muscle without visible stripes; its calcium regulation and activation patterns differ.

Nervous systems are diverse

Cnidarian nerve nets, ganglia and longitudinal nerve cords in many invertebrates, and vertebrate central/peripheral systems illustrate different organisations. Cephalisation concentrates sensory and integrating structures near a leading end in many bilaterally organised animals. These arrangements reflect body plans and behaviour, rather than a single scale of intelligence.

Sensory systems detect mechanical, chemical, light and other changes through specialised receptor processes. A receptor converts a stimulus into a cellular signal; subsequent neural pathways carry and interpret information. Adaptation of a sensory response to a continuing stimulus differs from evolutionary adaptation. Biological clocks add an internal timing component: daily rhythms can persist under constant conditions while environmental cues entrain their phase. State whether “adaptation” refers to receptor response, an individual’s adjustment or a heritable population feature.

Hormones work through responsive target cells

An endocrine hormone enters internal transport fluid and can reach distant cells. Only cells with suitable receptors and downstream machinery respond in the relevant way. Peptide hormones often signal through surface receptors; many steroid hormones act through intracellular receptors. The eventual response can involve enzyme activity, transport, gene expression or several of these together. “Hormones are always slow” is an unreliable rule: some effects appear rapidly, while others require longer changes in cellular state.

The response depends on secretion, transport, breakdown, receptor abundance and interactions with other signals. A high measured hormone concentration does not guarantee a large effect at every target. Neuroendocrine cells connect neural input to hormone release. Negative feedback can occur at several levels of a regulatory axis, allowing the state of a target process to influence upstream secretion. The reading on hormone action expands this distinction between signal and response.

Reproduction integrates timing, resources and feedback

Reproduction includes gamete production, fertilisation, development and the allocation of parental resources. Species vary in internal/external fertilisation, sex organisation, reproductive cycles and parental investment. A mammalian endocrine example provides a concrete feedback model without representing all animals.

Hypothalamic GnRH stimulates pituitary release of gonadotropins, including LH and FSH. These act on gonadal cells involved in steroid production and gametogenesis. Gonadal signals feed back on hypothalamic/pituitary activity. The sign and timing of feedback can change: sustained high oestrogen under appropriate conditions can contribute to positive feedback leading to an LH surge and ovulation. It would be incorrect to describe the entire reproductive cycle as one unchanging negative-feedback loop. In male mammals, gonadal and other signals also provide feedback governing endocrine activity and sperm production. Consult the supporting account of hormonal reproductive control.

Energy availability, environmental timing and stress can influence reproduction. These links explain why studying reproductive organs alone is insufficient. The circulation carries signals, tissues need fuel, and neural pathways provide information about surroundings. In a seasonal breeder, a prediction about reproductive timing should therefore consider environmental cues and physiological state rather than one hormone measurement alone.

Bring the animal half together

Use a terrestrial animal escaping a threat as a final integration case. Trace sensory detection, neural signalling, motor-unit recruitment, ATP use, oxygen transport, heat production and water costs. Then ask which processes can change immediately and which require sustained adjustment. For histology, use described muscle and neural features as pre-lab practice; actual tissue recognition must use the established slide collection. Your final explanation should show coordinated mechanisms and their limits, not merely name systems. Return to the animal route for revision.
