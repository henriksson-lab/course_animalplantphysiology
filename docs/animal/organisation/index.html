A1 — Organisation, diversity and regulation

Start with a physiological problem

A small aquatic animal and a mammal must both deliver oxygen to living cells. They face the same physical need but differ in body size, body organisation, environment and activity. Explaining their differences requires more than listing organs. We need to connect a structure to a process, identify the constraint that the structure addresses, and ask what evidence supports the explanation.

After this unit, you should be able to connect cells, tissues and organ systems; explain an exchange constraint using size and distance; recognise the four primary tissue categories from described features; construct a negative-feedback explanation; and distinguish an individual’s response from evolutionary change. Use the slides, then the exercises and self-check quiz. Each exercise page ends with bioinformatics and R exercises.

From cells to an organism

A cell is surrounded by a selectively permeable membrane. The membrane helps maintain differences between the cell and its surroundings. Transport proteins, metabolic reactions and signalling processes contribute to those differences. A multicellular animal adds extracellular fluid: most cells exchange with nearby interstitial fluid rather than directly with the outside environment. Blood, where present, transports materials between exchange organs and tissues. This arrangement means the circulation, respiratory surface and cell membrane are successive parts of one transport pathway.

A tissue combines cells and extracellular material in a functional arrangement. An organ contains several tissue types. In the intestine, an epithelial lining performs secretion and absorption, connective tissue supports it, smooth muscle moves its contents, and nervous tissue coordinates activity. An organ system groups interacting organs, but the boundaries of systems are teaching conveniences: digestion depends on circulation, nervous control and hormonal signals.

Draw an arrow from food in the gut to an absorbed nutrient in a muscle cell. The nutrient must cross the gut epithelium, enter a transport route and cross the muscle-cell membrane. Saying that digestion “gives the cells energy” skips these steps and also skips metabolism. Digestion releases absorbable molecules; cells subsequently transform chemical energy into ATP and other usable forms.

Why geometry matters

Transport across a surface depends on how much surface is available and how far material must move. To isolate the geometrical issue, imagine a cube with side length \(L\). Its surface area is \(6L^2\) and its volume is \(L^3\), so its surface-area-to-volume ratio is \(6/L\). The ratio has units of inverse length. For a cube of side 1 mm it is 6 mm⁻¹; for a cube of side 2 mm it is 3 mm⁻¹. Doubling length gives four times the surface but eight times the volume.

This does not mean that all large animals have low functional exchange areas. It explains why their exchange surfaces cannot simply be smooth scaled-up versions of a small body. Folding, branching and subdivision increase area; thin barriers shorten diffusion paths; bulk flow transports material across long distances. A mammal’s lungs and circulation solve different parts of the transport problem. Blood flow does not replace diffusion across the final barrier between a capillary and a tissue cell.

Diffusion over a long distance is slow. In a simple comparison with the same diffusing substance and conditions, the characteristic diffusion time grows with the square of distance. Increasing distance tenfold therefore makes this time scale roughly one hundredfold. This proportional argument explains the value of thin exchange barriers without requiring an exact diffusion coefficient. Real tissues also contain barriers, binding and flow, so the toy calculation is a mechanism-building aid rather than a complete physiological prediction.

Four tissue categories, many arrangements

For histology, start with what is visible and then make a functional inference. Epithelial tissue forms sheets and boundaries. Cells are closely associated, and one surface may face a lumen, meaning the inside space of a tube or cavity. An epithelium has an apical side and a basal side; their transport proteins and neighbours can differ. A thin single layer can support exchange, whereas several layers can provide protection. Neither function follows from thickness alone: location, junctions and specialised surfaces also matter.

Connective tissue contains cells within an extracellular matrix. The matrix may be flexible, strong, mineralised or fluid. Tendon, cartilage, bone and blood therefore differ substantially even though they belong to the same broad category. A connective-tissue description should mention matrix organisation rather than treating every pale space in a section as empty.

Muscle tissue develops force. Skeletal muscle typically has long striated fibres with multiple nuclei. Cardiac muscle has striated branching cells and specialised cell connections. Smooth muscle has spindle-shaped cells without the same visible striation pattern. These are useful recognition clues, but section angle and preparation affect appearance. Nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glial cells. Neurons receive, integrate and transmit signals; glia contribute to the environment in which those signals operate.

In an actual section, record scale, orientation, cell shape, nuclear position, layering, matrix and lumen relationships before proposing an identity. A circular profile could be a cross-section of a tube rather than a spherical structure. An organ name without supporting features is a guess. The descriptions used in our exercises practise reasoning; they do not replace identifying the actual course specimens. OpenStax provides openly accessible examples of the four primary tissue types.

Regulated variables and negative feedback

An animal survives within ranges of temperature, fluid composition and other conditions. Homeostasis is the active maintenance of suitable internal conditions despite disturbances. It is dynamic: material and energy can enter and leave while a measured variable remains approximately stable. Stability does not mean that every organ is inactive or that every variable is constant.

A useful feedback explanation identifies a regulated variable, a disturbance, a sensor, a control process, an effector and the consequence of the response. For example, increased body temperature can be detected by thermosensory systems. Neural control changes heat-loss or heat-production processes. Those changes can oppose the original rise in temperature. In negative feedback, the response reduces the deviation that helped trigger it.

The control process is not necessarily a single anatomical “control centre”, and a target range can change with time or physiological state. Positive feedback instead amplifies a change and needs some terminating condition. It can be useful during a finite event, but it is not the standard explanation for long-term stability. Feedback terminology refers to the direction of the loop, not whether its consequences are desirable.

This original functional sketch omits interacting pathways. Identify the opposing effect of the response, rather than inferring feedback merely from the diagram containing a loop.

Separate a controlled variable from an effector. In a thermal example, body temperature may be the regulated variable, while skin blood flow is one response that affects heat exchange. Observing increased blood flow does not establish that blood flow itself was the variable defended by the system. Use the homeostasis reading to compare another feedback loop with this example.

Comparison without a ladder of “better” animals

Different body plans face different constraints. A flat body can shorten distances to the environment, but it also limits possible geometry. A hard external covering can support and protect an animal while making exchange through the whole body surface more difficult. An insect’s tracheal system delivers gases through branching air-filled tubes, whereas a mammal uses blood for most oxygen delivery beyond the lung. These are alternative combinations of mechanisms, not stages on a ladder towards a mammal.

Evolutionary adaptation concerns heritable features shaped across generations. Acclimation concerns an individual’s adjustment to changed conditions, often under controlled conditions; acclimatisation is commonly used for adjustment in a natural setting. A fish changing its physiological performance after several weeks at a new temperature has not evolved during that experiment. A short-term response, such as changing ventilation within minutes, is also different from a sustained acclimatory change.

When explaining a trait, distinguish its current function from evidence about its evolutionary origin. A structure being useful does not by itself prove how it evolved. Comparisons are strongest when they connect environment, mechanism, performance and relevant evidence, while allowing for trade-offs and shared ancestry.
