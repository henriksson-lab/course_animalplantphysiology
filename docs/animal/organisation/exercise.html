A1 exercises — Structure, scale and feedback

Use the tutorial as a reference. These are paper-and-discussion tasks, not specimen identifications or instructions for animal experiments. Allow roughly 60–90 minutes including discussion. Leave with an annotated comparison, a tissue-evidence table and a feedback diagram.

1. Put the boundaries back

Draw a nutrient’s route from the mammalian gut lumen to the cytoplasm of a skeletal-muscle cell. Include the absorptive epithelial cell, internal transport fluid and muscle-cell membrane. Mark at least two places where selective membrane transport matters. Explain why finding glucose in the gut lumen does not establish that it has been absorbed.

Hint

The gut lumen is continuous with the exterior. Name what lies on both sides of each boundary; “goes into the body” is not a sufficiently precise arrow.

2. Work the small geometry case

For hypothetical cubic bodies with side lengths 1, 2 and 4 mm, calculate surface area, volume and surface-area-to-volume ratio. Give units for all three. Then predict how characteristic diffusion time changes when a transport distance rises from 0.1 to 1 mm, assuming the same medium and substance.

Explain why neither calculation proves that a large animal cannot exchange enough oxygen. Name two structural/process changes that the simple cube lacks. Show your arithmetic, not only the final ratio.

Numerical checkpoint

At 4 mm, area is 96 mm² and volume is 64 mm³. Check what happens when you divide them. The tenfold distance change concerns a squared relationship.

3. Recognise features before naming tissue

The following are invented descriptions, not observations from real slides. For each, propose a primary tissue category, two supporting features and one possible function. State what extra evidence would be needed to name an organ.

Description Features supplied
A One thin layer of closely associated cells around a lumen; little intervening matrix
B Widely spaced cells among abundant parallel extracellular fibres
C Long, parallel, striated fibres containing multiple nuclei
D Cell bodies with long processes among numerous smaller supporting cells

Now explain why a circular empty-looking profile alone cannot identify a blood vessel. Suggest how section angle could change a tubular structure’s appearance.

4. Repair a feedback explanation

A student writes: “When an animal gets hot, it sweats, so that is positive feedback because sweating is helpful.” Rewrite this account using a regulated variable, a disturbance, a sensor/control process, an effector and the effect on the original deviation. Then identify two limits: not all animals sweat, and evaporation depends on environmental conditions.

Draw the loop and label the arrow that opposes the initial disturbance.

5. Separate time scales and evidence

Classify each example as an acute response, an individual’s acclimatory change, or a proposed evolutionary adaptation. Explain the evidence needed for the last classification.

  1. Ventilation changes within minutes when an animal becomes active.
  2. A fish’s performance changes after several weeks at a new temperature.
  3. A heritable gill feature is proposed to improve performance in a population’s usual habitat over generations.

Would measuring a useful function in one individual be enough to establish the feature’s evolutionary origin? Explain.

6. Compare two solutions

Write 150–200 words comparing oxygen delivery in an insect and a mammal. Include the environmental medium, exchange pathway, internal transport route, two shared physical constraints and one trade-off. End with a measurement that could test part of your explanation. Avoid ranking the animals as “primitive” and “advanced”.

Finish with the self-check quiz.
