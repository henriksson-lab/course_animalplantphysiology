A1 exercises — Structure, scale and feedback

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Levels of organisation

1.1

Arrange from the smallest to the largest level: organ, organism, cell, organ system, tissue.

Answer

Cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.

1.2

In a multicellular animal, most cells exchange materials directly with:

  1. the external environment
  2. interstitial fluid
  3. blood plasma
  4. the gut lumen
Answer
  1. Interstitial (extracellular) fluid surrounds the cells; blood, where present, transports material between exchange organs and the tissues.

1.3

The intestinal wall contains all four primary tissue types. For each tissue type, give its main function in the intestine.

Answer
Tissue Function in the intestine
Epithelium Lines the lumen; secretion and absorption
Connective tissue Supports the epithelium and carries blood vessels
Smooth muscle Mixes and moves the contents
Nervous tissue Coordinates secretion and muscle activity

1.4

Put the steps in order for a glucose molecule moving from the gut lumen into a skeletal-muscle cell:

  1. crosses the muscle-cell membrane
  2. crosses the apical membrane of the intestinal epithelial cell
  3. is carried in the blood
  4. crosses the basolateral membrane of the intestinal epithelial cell
  5. enters the interstitial fluid around the muscle fibre
Answer

b → d → c → e → a. Glucose in the lumen has not yet been absorbed, because the lumen is continuous with the outside of the body.

Surface area, volume and diffusion

2.1

Complete the table for cubes with side length \(L\).

\(L\) (mm) Surface area (mm²) Volume (mm³) Surface area : volume (mm⁻¹)
1
2
4
Answer
\(L\) (mm) Surface area (mm²) Volume (mm³) Surface area : volume (mm⁻¹)
1 6 1 6
2 24 8 3
4 96 64 1.5

The ratio is \(6L^2 / L^3 = 6/L\), so each doubling of \(L\) halves it.

2.2

The time needed for diffusion is proportional to the square of the distance. By what factor does the diffusion time change when the distance increases from:

  1. 1 µm to 3 µm
  2. 0.1 mm to 1 mm
  3. 20 µm to 10 µm
Answer
  1. 3² = 9 times longer.
  2. 10² = 100 times longer.
  3. (1/2)² = 1/4, i.e. four times shorter.

2.3

Name three ways in which large animals overcome the low surface-area-to-volume ratio of a large body.

Answer
  1. Folding, branching or subdivision of exchange surfaces increases the area (e.g. alveoli, gill lamellae, intestinal villi).
  2. Thin exchange barriers shorten the diffusion distance.
  3. Bulk flow (ventilation and circulation) moves material over long distances.

Tissues

3.1

Name the primary tissue type that matches each description.

Description
A One layer of closely packed cells lining a lumen, with little material between the cells
B Widely spaced cells among abundant parallel extracellular fibres
C Long, cylindrical, striated fibres with many nuclei per fibre
D Cells with long processes, surrounded by many smaller supporting cells
Answer

A: epithelial tissue (simple epithelium). B: connective tissue (dense regular connective tissue, e.g. tendon). C: muscle tissue (skeletal muscle). D: nervous tissue (neurons and glial cells).

3.3

Which type of epithelium best suits each function: simple squamous or stratified squamous?

  1. gas exchange
  2. protection against abrasion
Answer
  1. Simple squamous: a single thin layer gives a short diffusion distance.
  2. Stratified squamous: several cell layers protect the tissue beneath.

3.4

Define the terms lumen, apical surface and basal surface.

Answer
  • Lumen: the inner space of a tube or cavity, such as the gut or a blood vessel.
  • Apical surface: the side of an epithelial cell that faces the lumen or the outside.
  • Basal surface: the side of an epithelial cell that faces the basement membrane and underlying tissue.

3.5

A section shows a circular profile with an empty centre. Why is this not enough to identify it as a blood vessel?

Answer

Any tube (such as a duct, gland, gut or vessel) cut in cross-section gives a ring. An oblique cut through the same tube gives an oval. Identification also needs the wall structure, its tissue layers and the scale.

Homeostasis and feedback

4.1

Define homeostasis.

Answer

Homeostasis is the active maintenance of internal conditions (such as temperature or the composition of extracellular fluid) within a range compatible with life, despite external and internal disturbances.

4.2

Body temperature in a mammal rises during exercise. Match each item to its role in the feedback loop: regulated variable, sensor, control centre, effector.

  1. sweat glands
  2. hypothalamus
  3. body temperature
  4. thermoreceptors
Answer
  1. effector; b. control centre; c. regulated variable; d. sensor.

4.3

State whether each example is negative or positive feedback.

  1. A rise in blood glucose increases insulin secretion, which lowers blood glucose.
  2. Uterine contractions increase oxytocin release, which strengthens the contractions.
  3. A rise in body temperature increases sweating.
  4. Activated clotting factors activate more clotting factors.
  5. A fall in blood pressure increases heart rate through the baroreceptor reflex.
Answer
  1. Negative. b. Positive. c. Negative. d. Positive. e. Negative.

4.4

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Positive feedback means that the response is good for the animal.
  2. In negative feedback, the response reduces the deviation that triggered it.
  3. Positive feedback must have a way to stop.
  4. A stable internal concentration means that no transport is taking place.
Answer
  1. False. “Positive” refers to the direction of the loop: the response amplifies the original change. It says nothing about benefit.
  2. True.
  3. True. Otherwise the change keeps growing, as in an action potential or during birth.
  4. False. Inputs and outputs can balance while transport continues.

Responses, acclimation and adaptation

5.1

Classify each example as an acute response, acclimation or evolutionary adaptation.

  1. Ventilation increases within seconds when an animal starts running.
  2. A fish kept at a higher temperature for four weeks changes its enzyme levels.
  3. A population of fish living in hypoxic water has inherited larger gill surfaces.
Answer
  1. Acute response. b. Acclimation. c. Evolutionary adaptation.

5.2

What is the difference between acclimation and acclimatisation?

Answer

Both are reversible changes within one individual. Acclimation describes the response to a change in one condition, usually in the laboratory. Acclimatisation describes the response to changes in the natural environment.

Explore: Diffusion limits body size

The demo shows a ball (or flat sheet) of tissue in air-saturated water that gets O₂ only by diffusion from its surface. Colour shows the O₂ level and the readouts give A/V, the diffusion time to the centre, the largest size that diffusion can supply and the O₂ at the centre. Use the radius and O₂-use sliders, or the preset buttons.

E1

Press Reset (sphere, radius 0.50 mm, O₂ use 0.20 mL g⁻¹ h⁻¹). Read off:

  1. A/V
  2. the diffusion time to the centre
  3. the O₂ at the centre, as % of the surface value
Answer
  1. 6.00 mm⁻¹ (= 3/r for a sphere).
  2. About 62 s (\(t \approx x^2/2D\) with \(x\) = 0.5 mm and \(D\) = 2 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹).
  3. 81 %. Every cell gets O₂.

E2

From Reset, drag the radius up to 1.0 mm.

  1. By what factor has A/V changed?
  2. By what factor has the diffusion time to the centre changed?
  3. What does the readout give as the largest radius that diffusion alone can supply?
Answer
  1. It halves, from 6.00 to 3.00 mm⁻¹ (A/V ∝ 1/r).
  2. It increases four times, from about 62 s to 250 s (shown as 4 min), because diffusion time ∝ r².
  3. 1.14 mm. Above this radius an anoxic core appears; at 1.0 mm the centre still has about 23 % O₂.

E3

Press 5 mm ball. What percentage of the tissue gets no O₂?

Answer

About 64 %. The radius is more than four times the largest radius (1.14 mm) that diffusion can supply, so most of the volume is anoxic.

E4

Press Mouse tissue (radius 0.50 mm, O₂ use 2.5 mL g⁻¹ h⁻¹, about 12 times the Reset value).

  1. What is now the largest radius that diffusion can supply?
  2. Which statement explains the change? (i) the largest radius falls in proportion to the O₂ use; (ii) the largest radius falls in proportion to the square root of the O₂ use; (iii) the largest radius does not depend on the O₂ use.
Answer
  1. 323 µm (0.32 mm), so the 0.50 mm ball now has an anoxic core (17 % of the tissue).
  2. (ii). The largest radius is \(\sqrt{6DC_0/M}\); 12.5 times the O₂ use gives \(\sqrt{12.5} \approx 3.5\) times smaller radius (1.14 mm → 0.32 mm). A tissue with a mammalian metabolic rate must therefore be supplied over much shorter distances, by capillaries.

E5

Press Flatworm (flat sheet, 0.15 mm half-thickness).

  1. What is the largest half-thickness that diffusion can supply?
  2. Does this limit depend on the length and width of the sheet?
Answer
  1. 660 µm (0.66 mm), i.e. a sheet up to about 1.3 mm thick. For a flat sheet the limit is \(\sqrt{2DC_0/M}\), which is \(1/\sqrt{3}\) of the sphere’s limit.
  2. No. Only the thickness sets the diffusion distance, so a flatworm can be several centimetres long and wide as long as it stays thin.

Explore: Feedback control of body temperature

The demo simulates body temperature under a heat load from exercise (or cold) between minute 10 and minute 40. The effector response is proportional to the deviation from the 37 °C set point. Choose the type of loop with the buttons, set the gain, response delay and disturbance with the sliders, and read the deviations in the readout.

E6

Press Reset (negative feedback, gain 0.5, no delay, disturbance +0.30 °C min⁻¹).

  1. What is the deviation from the set point at minute 40?
  2. Is the temperature back at 37 ± 0.1 °C at 60 min?
Answer
  1. +0.55 °C. Some deviation remains during the whole exercise period, because the effector response is driven by the deviation itself.
  2. Yes.

E7

Keep negative feedback and set the gain to 1.0, then to 2.0 (Strong, fast). Read the deviation at minute 40 each time. What happens to the remaining error when the gain is doubled?

Answer

Gain 1.0: +0.29 °C. Gain 2.0: +0.15 °C. Doubling the gain roughly halves the remaining error; a stronger response holds the regulated variable closer to the set point, but never exactly on it.

E8

Return to Reset and press None, then Positive feedback. For each, read the deviation at minute 40 and say whether the temperature is back at 37 °C by 60 min.

Answer
Loop Deviation at minute 40 Back at 37 °C at 60 min?
None +4.66 °C No
Positive feedback +13.00 °C (runs off the top of the plot) No

Without feedback the heat load drives a drift of several degrees. With positive feedback the response amplifies the deviation, so temperature runs away out of the survivable range and does not return when exercise stops.

E9

Press Strong, slow (gain 2.0, delay 1.5 min). Then lower the gain to 0.5 while keeping the 1.5 min delay.

  1. What happens with gain 2.0 and the delay?
  2. What is the largest deviation with gain 0.5 and the delay?
Answer
  1. The temperature overshoots the set point and oscillates with growing amplitude until it leaves the survivable range: a strong response that acts on old information over-corrects.
  2. +0.69 °C (compared with +0.55 °C without delay). With a weaker response the delay causes only a small overshoot, and temperature returns to 37 °C.

E10

Press Cold (disturbance −0.30 °C min⁻¹). What is the deviation at minute 40, and is the effector cooling or heating the body?

Answer

−0.55 °C, the mirror image of Reset. The effector trace lies below the line: it is heating (for example shivering), opposing the fall in temperature.

Bioinformatics and R

These exercises use UniProtKB, the main database of protein sequences and their functions, together with R. Each exercise is a separate small problem about how body size and tissue organisation show up in real data. Run the R code in R or RStudio; only base R and the MASS package (installed with every copy of R) are used. The code reads sequences directly from the internet, so you need a network connection.

How to search UniProtKB

Go to https://www.uniprot.org and type a query in the search box. Plain words (myoglobin) search every field, which gives many unwanted hits. Field queries are more precise and can be combined with AND, OR and NOT:

Query Finds
gene:MB entries whose gene name or gene-name synonym is MB
protein_name:myoglobin entries with “myoglobin” in the protein name
organism_id:9606 human (NCBI taxonomy number); mouse is 10090
organism_name:"sperm whale" organisms whose name contains the phrase
reviewed:true only Swiss-Prot entries, curated by experts

Example: gene:MB AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true.

Reviewed (Swiss-Prot, gold star icon) entries are checked by curators and there is normally one per protein and species. Unreviewed (TrEMBL) entries are computer-annotated and often fragments or duplicates. Use the Reviewed filter in the left-hand panel, or add reviewed:true.

Each entry has a stable accession (for example P02144) and an entry name (MYG_HUMAN). Cite the accession: it does not change between releases. An entry page is divided into sections: Function, Names & Taxonomy, Subcellular Location, PTM/Processing (with the Features table giving positions such as the mature chain), Family & Domains and Sequence. The sequence in FASTA format is at https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/<accession>.fasta; R can read that address with readLines().

B1. Finding the right myoglobin

Myoglobin is the oxygen-binding protein of muscle. You want the myoglobin of three mammals that differ about a million-fold in body mass: mouse, human and sperm whale. Use UniProtKB.

  1. Search gene:MB AND organism_id:9606. Many hits appear, most of them short. Tick the Reviewed filter. What is the accession of the reviewed human myoglobin entry?
  2. Search gene:MB AND organism_id:10090 AND reviewed:true (mouse). The reviewed list contains more than myoglobin, for example the B-cell protein CD79A. Why?
    1. CD79A is a myoglobin that is expressed in blood cells
    2. gene: also matches gene-name synonyms, and CD79A has the old synonym Mb-1
    3. The search ignores the organism filter
    4. Unreviewed entries were not removed
  3. Search myoglobin AND organism_name:"sperm whale" AND reviewed:true. Three reviewed entries appear, from three different whale species. Which accession belongs to the sperm whale, Physeter macrocephalus (taxonomy number 9755)? Which query would have given only this entry?
Answer
  1. P02144 (entry name MYG_HUMAN, 154 amino acids). The unreviewed hits are mostly automatically predicted fragments from alternative transcripts.
  2. 2. Field gene: searches gene names and their synonyms. Always check the protein name and organism of a hit, not only that it matched.
  3. P02185 (MYG_PHYMC). The others are the dwarf (Kogia sima, P02184) and pygmy sperm whale (Kogia breviceps, Q0KIY5), whose common names also contain “sperm whale”. gene:MB AND organism_id:9755 AND reviewed:true (or protein_name:myoglobin AND organism_id:9755) gives only P02185: a taxonomy number is unambiguous where a common name is not.

B2. What does myoglobin do in a muscle fibre?

Open the human myoglobin entry, https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/P02144/entry.

  1. In the Function section, myoglobin’s primary function is to store oxygen and to … (choose one)
    1. carry oxygen in blood plasma between lungs and tissues
    2. facilitate the diffusion of oxygen within muscle tissue
    3. pump oxygen across the plasma membrane
  2. In Subcellular Location, where in the cell is myoglobin found?
  3. In the Features table (section PTM/Processing) the chain “Myoglobin” runs from position 2 to 154. How many amino acids does the mature protein have, and what happened to residue 1?
  4. In the Function section’s features, which amino acid is at position 94 (the proximal residue binding heme), and what does it hold in place?
Answer
  1. 2. Myoglobin stores O₂ and speeds its movement from the capillary side of a muscle fibre to the mitochondria, which helps with the diffusion limit over distances of tens of micrometres discussed in this unit.
  2. In the cytoplasm of the muscle cell, the sarcoplasm. Unlike haemoglobin it is not in blood: it works inside the tissue, on the last part of the oxygen path.
  3. 153 amino acids. The initiator methionine (residue 1) is removed after translation, which is why the mature chain starts at position 2.
  4. Histidine (H94). It binds the iron of the heme group; oxygen binds on the other side of the heme, near another histidine at position 65.

B3. Is a whale’s myoglobin a scaled-up mouse myoglobin?

A sperm whale is roughly a million times heavier than a mouse. Do its muscle proteins also differ in size? Read the three FASTA files from B1 into R and compare them. All three proteins have the same length, so the residues can be compared position by position without an alignment program.

read_uniprot <- function(acc) {
  url   <- paste0("https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/", acc, ".fasta")
  lines <- readLines(url)
  cat(lines[1], "\n")                      # the header line
  paste(lines[-1], collapse = "")          # join the sequence lines
}
human <- read_uniprot("P02144")
mouse <- read_uniprot("P04247")
whale <- read_uniprot("P02185")
nchar(c(human = human, mouse = mouse, whale = whale))

h <- strsplit(human, "")[[1]]              # one letter per residue
m <- strsplit(mouse, "")[[1]]
w <- strsplit(whale, "")[[1]]
identity <- function(a, b) round(100 * mean(a == b), 1)   # % identical positions
c(human_mouse = identity(h, m),
  human_whale = identity(h, w),
  mouse_whale = identity(m, w))
sum(h == m & h == w)                       # positions identical in all three
c(human = h[c(65, 94)], mouse = m[c(65, 94)], whale = w[c(65, 94)])
  1. How long is myoglobin in each species?
  2. Which pair of species is the least similar, and what is its percentage identity?
  3. At how many of the positions are all three species identical?
  4. Are the two oxygen- and heme-binding histidines (positions 65 and 94) conserved in all three?
Answer 
>sp|P02144|MYG_HUMAN Myoglobin OS=Homo sapiens OX=9606 GN=MB PE=1 SV=2 
>sp|P04247|MYG_MOUSE Myoglobin OS=Mus musculus OX=10090 GN=Mb PE=1 SV=3 
>sp|P02185|MYG_PHYMC Myoglobin OS=Physeter macrocephalus OX=9755 GN=MB PE=1 SV=2 
human mouse whale 
  154   154   154 
human_mouse human_whale mouse_whale 
       83.8        84.4        79.9 
[1] 118
human1 human2 mouse1 mouse2 whale1 whale2 
   "H"    "H"    "H"    "H"    "H"    "H"

  2. Mouse and sperm whale, 79.9 %. Human is about equally close to both (about 84 %).

  4. Yes, both histidines are present in all three.

Body size scales organs, not molecules. A protein that does one job (holding oxygen on a heme) keeps its size and its key residues across a million-fold range of body mass. Large divers differ instead in how much myoglobin their muscles contain and in some surface residues.

B4. Do big mammals have proportionally big brains?

MASS::mammals holds real average body mass (kg) and brain mass (g) of 62 mammal species. If brain mass were proportional to body mass, the slope of log(brain) against log(body) would be 1. A slope of 2/3 would match the surface-area rule used in this unit.

library(MASS)                     # ships with R
head(mammals)
fit <- lm(log10(brain) ~ log10(body), data = mammals)
round(coef(fit), 3)
round(confint(fit), 3)           # 95 % confidence intervals
plot(log10(mammals$body), log10(mammals$brain),
     xlab = "log10 body mass (kg)", ylab = "log10 brain mass (g)")
abline(fit)
pct <- 100 * (mammals$brain / 1000) / mammals$body   # brain as % of body mass
names(pct) <- rownames(mammals)
round(pct[c("Mouse", "Human", "African elephant")], 3)
  1. What is the slope, to three decimals?
  2. Does its 95 % confidence interval include 1? Does it include 2/3 (0.667)?
  3. What percentage of body mass is brain in the mouse and in the African elephant?
Answer 
(Intercept) log10(body) 
      0.927       0.752 
            2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept) 0.844  1.011
log10(body) 0.695  0.809
           Mouse            Human African elephant 
           1.739            2.129            0.086
  1. 0.752, so brain mass ∝ body mass^0.75.
  2. No and no. The interval 0.695–0.809 excludes both 1 and 0.667. Brain mass rises less steeply than body mass, but more steeply than simple surface area; it is close to the 3/4 exponent often found for whole-body metabolic rate.
  3. Mouse 1.739 %, African elephant 0.086 %. On a log–log plot a slope below 1 means that each organ makes up a smaller fraction of a larger body, just as surface area per unit volume falls when a cube gets bigger.

B5. Which mammal has the largest brain for its size?

The line from B4 predicts the brain mass expected for a given body mass. A residual on the log₁₀ scale converts to a ratio: 10^residual is observed brain mass divided by expected brain mass. Run this after the code in B4.

ratio <- 10^residuals(fit)                 # observed / expected brain mass
round(sort(ratio, decreasing = TRUE)[1:5], 1)
round(sort(ratio)[1:3], 2)
mammals[c("Human", "African elephant"), ]
  1. Which species has the highest ratio, and how many times larger is its brain than expected for its body mass?
  2. Which species has the lowest ratio?
  3. Which species in the data has the heaviest brain in absolute terms?
Answer 
        Human Rhesus monkey        Baboon    Owl monkey    Chimpanzee 
          7.0           5.0           3.6           3.2           2.7 
Water opossum        Tenrec    Musk shrew 
         0.18          0.33          0.38 
                   body brain
Human              62  1320
African elephant 6654  5712
  1. Human, 7.0 times the expected brain mass. The next four are also primates.
  2. Water opossum (0.18 of expected).
  3. African elephant (5712 g against 1320 g in humans). Absolute size and size relative to the scaling line answer different questions: only the second removes the effect of body size, which is why comparative physiologists compare residuals rather than raw values.

B6. The protein that makes connective tissue strong

Connective tissue is defined by its extracellular matrix. In tendon and bone the main matrix protein is collagen type I. Search UniProtKB for gene:COL1A1 AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true and open the entry.

  1. What is the accession?
  2. In Subcellular Location, where does the protein end up?
