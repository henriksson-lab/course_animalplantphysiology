A4 exercises — Water, ions and excretion

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Osmotic concentration and tonicity

1.1

Calculate the osmolarity (mOsm L⁻¹) of each solution. Treat the solutions as ideal and the salts as fully dissociated.

  1. 0.15 mol L⁻¹ glucose
  2. 0.15 mol L⁻¹ NaCl
  3. 0.10 mol L⁻¹ CaCl₂
  4. 0.10 mol L⁻¹ NaCl + 0.10 mol L⁻¹ urea
Answer
  1. 150 mOsm L⁻¹ (glucose does not dissociate).
  2. 300 mOsm L⁻¹ (Na⁺ + Cl⁻: 2 particles per formula unit).
  3. 300 mOsm L⁻¹ (Ca²⁺ + 2 Cl⁻: 3 particles per formula unit).
  4. 300 mOsm L⁻¹ (200 from NaCl + 100 from urea).

1.2

An animal’s body fluids have an osmolarity of 300 mOsm L⁻¹. For each external medium, state whether the medium is hyperosmotic, isosmotic or hyposmotic to the body fluids, and the direction in which water tends to move across a water-permeable body surface.

  1. 10 mOsm L⁻¹ (fresh water)
  2. 300 mOsm L⁻¹
  3. 1,000 mOsm L⁻¹ (seawater)
Answer
  1. Hyposmotic; water tends to enter the animal.
  2. Isosmotic; no net osmotic water movement.
  3. Hyperosmotic; water tends to leave the animal.

1.3

Red blood cells (intracellular osmolarity 300 mOsm L⁻¹) are placed in each solution. State whether the cells swell, shrink or keep their volume. The membrane is permeable to urea but effectively impermeable to NaCl.

  1. 300 mOsm L⁻¹ NaCl
  2. 150 mOsm L⁻¹ NaCl
  3. 600 mOsm L⁻¹ NaCl
  4. 300 mOsm L⁻¹ urea
Answer
  1. Keep their volume (isotonic).
  2. Swell (hypotonic).
  3. Shrink (hypertonic).
  4. Swell, and eventually burst. Urea enters the cells until its concentration is equal on both sides, so it exerts no lasting osmotic effect. The cells’ own trapped solutes then draw water in. The solution is isosmotic but hypotonic.

1.4

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Two solutions with the same osmolarity always have the same effect on cell volume.
  2. Osmolarity depends on the number of dissolved particles, not on their mass.
  3. An osmoconformer keeps every internal ion at the same concentration as seawater.
  4. A hydrostatic pressure can stop net osmotic water movement before the solute concentrations on the two sides become equal.
Answer
  1. False. Tonicity depends only on solutes that cannot cross the membrane, so isosmotic solutions can differ in tonicity (see 1.3 d).
  2. True.
  3. False. An osmoconformer matches the total osmotic concentration of its medium but may still regulate individual ions.
  4. True.

Fish in fresh water and seawater

2.1

Complete the table for typical teleost (bony) fishes.

Freshwater teleost Marine teleost
Body fluids compared with the medium
Passive water movement
Passive salt movement
Drinking
Urine volume and concentration
Direction of active NaCl transport at the gills
Answer
Freshwater teleost Marine teleost
Body fluids compared with the medium Hyperosmotic Hyposmotic
Passive water movement Inward (gain) Outward (loss)
Passive salt movement Outward (loss) Inward (gain)
Drinking Little or none Drinks seawater

Food also supplies ions to the freshwater fish. In the marine fish, the kidney excretes most of the excess divalent ions (Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻).

2.2

Marine sharks have a body-fluid osmolarity slightly above that of seawater.

  1. Which two organic solutes do they retain to raise their osmolarity?
  2. In which direction does water tend to move across their gills?
  3. Which organ secretes their excess NaCl?
Answer
  1. Urea and trimethylamine oxide (TMAO). TMAO counteracts the destabilising effect of urea on proteins.
  2. Slightly inward, so sharks do not need to drink seawater.
  3. The rectal gland.

Epithelial transport

3.1

In a typical transporting epithelial cell:

  1. On which membrane, apical or basolateral, is the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase located?
  2. How many Na⁺ and K⁺ ions does it move per ATP, and in which directions?
  3. Why is the pump needed for Na⁺ to enter the cell passively across the other membrane?
Answer
  1. The basolateral membrane.
  2. 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ into the cell per ATP hydrolysed.
  3. By removing Na⁺, the pump keeps intracellular Na⁺ low (and the inside negative). This creates an inward electrochemical gradient for Na⁺ across the apical membrane, which channels and cotransporters use.

3.2

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. The kidney reabsorbs water by active transport of water molecules.
  2. Water crosses epithelia through aquaporins and between cells, following an osmotic gradient created by solute transport.
  3. A list of transporters is enough to predict the direction of net transport across an epithelium.
Answer
  1. False. No pump transports water. Water follows osmotic gradients set up by active solute transport.
  2. True.
  3. False. You also need to know whether each transporter is on the apical or basolateral membrane.

The nephron

4.1

Put the structures in the order that filtrate passes through them.

  1. collecting duct
  2. distal convoluted tubule
  3. Bowman’s capsule
  4. thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle
  5. proximal convoluted tubule
  6. descending limb of the loop of Henle
  7. renal pelvis
Answer

c → e → f → d → b → a → g.

4.2

Match each nephron segment (1–5) with its main transport function (A–E).

Segment
1 Proximal convoluted tubule
2 Descending limb of the loop of Henle
3 Thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle
4 Distal convoluted tubule
5 Collecting duct
Function
A Reabsorbs NaCl (Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter) but is impermeable to water, so the tubular fluid becomes dilute
B Water reabsorption regulated by ADH; Na⁺ reabsorption regulated by aldosterone
C Reabsorbs about two thirds of the filtered Na⁺ and water, and nearly all filtered glucose and amino acids
D Permeable to water but has low permeability to NaCl; water leaves into the hyperosmotic medulla
E Fine-tunes reabsorption of NaCl and Ca²⁺; its fluid is still hyposmotic to plasma
Answer

1–C, 2–D, 3–A, 4–E, 5–B.

4.3

Define filtration, reabsorption, secretion and excretion in the kidney, stating the direction of movement for each.

Answer
  • Filtration: bulk flow of plasma water and small solutes from the glomerular capillaries into Bowman’s capsule.
  • Reabsorption: movement of a substance from the tubular fluid back into the blood.
  • Secretion: movement of a substance from the blood (via the interstitial fluid) into the tubular fluid.
  • Excretion: the amount of a substance that leaves the body in the urine.

4.4

Name the three layers of the glomerular filtration barrier, and state which plasma components are normally almost absent from the filtrate.

Answer
  1. Fenestrated capillary endothelium.
  2. Glomerular basement membrane.
  3. Filtration slits between the foot processes of podocytes.

Blood cells and large plasma proteins such as albumin are almost absent from the filtrate.

Renal calculations

5.1

For a freely filtered solute, the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is 0.10 L min⁻¹ and the plasma concentration is 5 mmol L⁻¹. Reabsorption is 0.40 mmol min⁻¹ and secretion is 0.02 mmol min⁻¹. Urine flow is 0.002 L min⁻¹.

  1. Calculate the filtered load.
  2. Calculate the excretion rate.
  3. Calculate the urine concentration of the solute.
  4. Urine flow is halved while the excretion rate stays the same. What is the new urine concentration?
Answer
  1. Filtered load = GFR × plasma concentration = 0.10 L min⁻¹ × 5 mmol L⁻¹ = 0.50 mmol min⁻¹.
  2. Excretion = 0.50 − 0.40 + 0.02 = 0.12 mmol min⁻¹.
  3. Urine concentration = excretion ÷ urine flow = 0.12 ÷ 0.002 = 60 mmol L⁻¹.
  4. 0.12 ÷ 0.001 = 120 mmol L⁻¹. The concentration doubles although the amount excreted per minute is unchanged.

5.2

The renal clearance of a substance is \(C = U \times V / P\), where \(U\) is its urine concentration, \(V\) the urine flow and \(P\) its plasma concentration. Inulin is infused into a subject. Plasma inulin is 0.20 mg mL⁻¹, urine inulin is 25 mg mL⁻¹ and urine flow is 1.0 mL min⁻¹.

  1. Calculate the inulin clearance.
  2. Why does inulin clearance equal the GFR?
Answer
  1. \(C = 25 \times 1.0 / 0.20 = 125\) mL min⁻¹.
  2. Inulin is freely filtered and is neither reabsorbed, secreted nor metabolised by the tubule. All filtered inulin is therefore excreted, and the volume of plasma cleared per minute equals the volume filtered per minute.

5.3

The GFR is 125 mL min⁻¹. Four freely filtered substances have the clearances below. For each, state whether the tubule shows net reabsorption, net secretion or neither.

  1. Glucose: 0 mL min⁻¹
  2. Urea: 60 mL min⁻¹
  3. Substance X: 125 mL min⁻¹
  4. Para-aminohippuric acid (PAH): 600 mL min⁻¹
Answer
  1. Net reabsorption (complete).
  2. Net reabsorption (partial).
  3. Neither, like inulin.
  4. Net secretion.

Clearance below the GFR means net reabsorption; clearance above the GFR means net secretion.

5.4

A person has a GFR of 125 mL min⁻¹ and a plasma glucose concentration of 5 mmol L⁻¹.

  1. Calculate the filtered load of glucose in mmol min⁻¹.
  2. The maximum rate of tubular glucose reabsorption (transport maximum, \(T_m\)) is 2.0 mmol min⁻¹. How much glucose is excreted at this plasma concentration?
  3. In uncontrolled diabetes, plasma glucose rises to 20 mmol L⁻¹. Calculate the glucose excretion rate.
Answer
  1. 0.125 L min⁻¹ × 5 mmol L⁻¹ = 0.625 mmol min⁻¹.
  2. None. The filtered load is below \(T_m\), so all of it is reabsorbed.
  3. Filtered load = 0.125 × 20 = 2.5 mmol min⁻¹. Excretion = 2.5 − 2.0 = 0.5 mmol min⁻¹.

5.5

A human kidney filters 180 L of plasma per day and produces 1.5 L of urine per day. What percentage of the filtered water is reabsorbed?

Answer

(180 − 1.5) / 180 × 100 = 99.2 %.

Concentrating the urine

6.1

Put the steps in order for the response to dehydration.

  1. Aquaporin-2 water channels are inserted into the apical membrane of collecting-duct cells.
  2. Plasma osmolarity rises.
  3. ADH binds to V2 receptors on collecting-duct cells.
  4. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the change.
  5. Water moves from the collecting duct into the hyperosmotic medullary interstitium, and a small volume of concentrated urine is produced.
  6. ADH is released from the posterior pituitary.
  7. Plasma osmolarity falls and ADH release decreases.
Answer

b → d → f → c → a → e → g. Step g is the negative feedback.

6.2

A person must excrete 600 mOsm of solute per day. The maximum urine osmolarity in humans is about 1,200 mOsm L⁻¹.

  1. What is the minimum daily urine volume?
  2. A person who lacks ADH (diabetes insipidus) produces urine of 100 mOsm L⁻¹. What is their daily urine volume for the same solute load?
Answer
  1. 600 mOsm ÷ 1,200 mOsm L⁻¹ = 0.5 L day⁻¹.
  2. 600 mOsm ÷ 100 mOsm L⁻¹ = 6 L day⁻¹.

6.3

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Tubular fluid leaving the thick ascending limb is hyposmotic to plasma.
  2. ADH increases urine concentration mainly by reducing the GFR.
  3. Without ADH, the collecting duct has low water permeability and the urine stays dilute.
  4. Urea contributes to the osmotic gradient of the inner medulla.
  5. Desert rodents that produce very concentrated urine tend to have relatively long loops of Henle.
Answer
  1. True (about 100 mOsm L⁻¹), because NaCl is removed without water.
  2. False. ADH increases the water permeability of the collecting duct, so more water is reabsorbed into the medulla.
  3. True.
  4. True.
  5. True. A longer loop (and a thicker medulla) builds a larger corticomedullary gradient.

7.2

Classify each animal as ammonotelic, ureotelic or uricotelic.

  1. goldfish
  2. human
  3. chicken
  4. tadpole
  5. adult frog
  6. desert locust
Answer
  1. Ammonotelic. b. Ureotelic. c. Uricotelic. d. Ammonotelic.
  2. Ureotelic. f. Uricotelic.

7.3

Why is uric acid, rather than urea, the nitrogenous waste of embryos that develop inside a shelled (cleidoic) egg?

Answer

The egg is a closed system: wastes cannot leave it. Uric acid is insoluble, so it precipitates and is stored in solid form, where it is neither toxic nor osmotically active. Urea would stay dissolved and accumulate to toxic concentrations.

7.4

Put the steps of urine formation in an insect in order.

  1. Water, ions and useful solutes are reabsorbed in the rectum.
  2. K⁺ is actively secreted into the lumen of the Malpighian tubules.
  3. Dry waste, including precipitated uric acid, is eliminated with the faeces.
  4. Water and solutes, including uric acid, follow into the tubule lumen.
  5. The tubule fluid empties into the hindgut.
Answer

b → d → e → a → c. The Malpighian tubules produce a fluid isosmotic to the haemolymph by secretion, not by filtration; the hindgut recovers water.

Explore: Tonicity and cell volume

A red cell with 300 mOsm L⁻¹ of trapped solute sits in a bath of NaCl, which cannot cross the membrane, and urea, which can. Set the bath with the sliders or presets and watch the cell volume over time; the readout gives the total and the non-penetrating osmolarity outside and the final cell volume.

E1

The demo opens with NaCl 150 + urea 150 mOsm L⁻¹. Read off the total osmolarity outside, the non-penetrating osmolarity, the verdict and the final cell volume.

Answer

Total 300 mOsm L⁻¹, non-penetrating 150 mOsm L⁻¹, “isosmotic, but hypotonic”, final volume 1.67×. Urea diffuses into the cell and water follows, so only the NaCl counts for tonicity.

E2

Press NaCl 200 and then NaCl 400. Give the final cell volume for each and say whether each bath is hypotonic, isotonic or hypertonic.

Answer

NaCl 200: 1.35×, hypotonic (the cell swells). NaCl 400: 0.83×, hypertonic (the cell shrinks and crenates). With only non-penetrating solute, osmolarity and tonicity agree.

E3

Press Urea 300. Which is the outcome?

  1. The cell keeps its volume, because the bath is isosmotic.
  2. The cell shrinks, because urea draws water out.
  3. The cell swells until it bursts.
Answer
  1. The bath is isosmotic (300 mOsm L⁻¹) but its non-penetrating osmolarity is 0, so it is as hypotonic as pure water: urea and water both enter until the cell bursts (haemolysis).

E4

Press NaCl 150 + urea 150 and untick Membrane lets urea through. Give the final cell volume and the verdict. What does the comparison with E1 show?

  1. Tonicity depends on the solution alone.
  2. Tonicity depends on the solution and on the permeability of the membrane.
Answer

Final volume 1.00×, “isosmotic and isotonic”. b: the same bath is hypotonic to a urea-permeable cell and isotonic to a urea-impermeable one.

E5

Tick the urea box again and set NaCl 300 + urea 300 mOsm L⁻¹ with the sliders. Before running it, predict the final volume: smaller than 1, 1.00, or larger than 1? Then check the verdict and the final volume.

Answer

1.00×, “hyperosmotic, but isotonic” (total 600, non-penetrating 300 mOsm L⁻¹). The cell shrinks briefly while water leaves faster than urea enters, then recovers as urea equilibrates.

Explore: Glucose transport maximum

The kidney filters glucose at a rate GFR × plasma glucose and reabsorbs it up to a transport maximum, Tm. Change plasma glucose, GFR and Tm with the sliders or presets; the bar shows one minute of filtered glucose split into reabsorbed (green) and excreted (gold), and the readout gives the rates and the glucose clearance.

E6

Press Fasting. Read off the threshold, the filtered load, the excreted glucose and the glucose clearance.

Answer

Threshold 16.0 mmol L⁻¹ (= Tm ÷ GFR = 2 ÷ 0.125 L min⁻¹). Filtered load 0.63 mmol min⁻¹ (0.125 × 5), all reabsorbed: excreted 0.00 mmol min⁻¹, clearance 0 mL min⁻¹.

E7

Press Untreated diabetes (plasma glucose 20 mmol L⁻¹). Read off the filtered, reabsorbed and excreted glucose and the clearance.

Answer

Filtered 2.50, reabsorbed 2.00 (= Tm), excreted 0.50 mmol min⁻¹. Clearance 0.50 ÷ 20 mmol L⁻¹ = 0.025 L min⁻¹ = 25 mL min⁻¹, below the GFR of 125, because glucose is reabsorbed.

E8

Press Diabetes + low GFR (GFR 60 mL min⁻¹, plasma glucose still 20 mmol L⁻¹). Read off the threshold and the excreted glucose. Which statement is correct?

  1. Glucose disappears from the urine because the patient’s diabetes is now controlled.
  2. Glucose disappears from the urine because the filtered load (1.20 mmol min⁻¹) is now below Tm.
  3. Glucose excretion doubles because less of the filtrate is reabsorbed.
Answer

Threshold 33.3 mmol L⁻¹, excreted 0.00 mmol min⁻¹. b: with a low GFR, less glucose is filtered than the tubule can take back, so a negative urine test does not rule out high plasma glucose in kidney disease.

E9

Press Fasting, then set Tm to 1.0 mmol min⁻¹, as with a drug that blocks tubular glucose transporters. Read off the new threshold. Then set plasma glucose to 10 mmol L⁻¹ and read off the excreted glucose.

Answer

Threshold 8.0 mmol L⁻¹ (1.0 ÷ 0.125). At 10 mmol L⁻¹: filtered 1.25, reabsorbed 1.00, excreted 0.25 mmol min⁻¹. Halving Tm halves the threshold, so glucose appears in the urine at a plasma concentration that was previously fully reabsorbed.

E10

Set plasma glucose to 30 mmol L⁻¹, GFR to 180 mL min⁻¹ and Tm to 2.0 mmol min⁻¹. Read off the clearance. Can any combination of the sliders make the glucose clearance larger than the GFR?

Answer

Clearance 113 mL min⁻¹ (excreted 3.40 mmol min⁻¹ ÷ 30 mmol L⁻¹), below the GFR of 180. No: glucose is only filtered and reabsorbed, never secreted, so the excreted amount can never exceed the filtered load and clearance stays below GFR.

Bioinformatics and R

Some people pass 5–10 litres of dilute urine a day although their blood contains plenty of ADH. This condition, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (NDI), means that the kidney cannot respond to the hormone. The exercises below use it as a running example. The new skills in this unit are looking at protein 3D structures (experimental structures in the RCSB PDB and predicted models in the AlphaFold database, with their pLDDT confidence) and looking up disease variants (the UniProt Disease & Variants section and ClinVar). R is used to read real structure and variant files from the databases and to model the urine volume. Only base R is used; run the code in R or RStudio with an internet connection.

How to search structure and variant databases

RCSB PDB (https://www.rcsb.org) stores structures that were solved by experiment. Type a protein name (aquaporin 2) or a UniProt accession (P41181) in the search box. Use the filters on the left to keep only Homo sapiens. Each 4-character PDB ID (for example 4NEF) is one deposited structure. Its entry page shows:

  • Experimental method: X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy (cryo-EM) or NMR.
  • Resolution in ångström (Å): the smaller the number, the finer the detail. About 2 Å shows individual side chains clearly; 4 Å shows the shape of the helices.
  • Macromolecules: which protein chains are in the model and how many copies (chains A, B, C …). Several copies often mean that the protein works as an oligomer.
  • Sequence tab: which residues were actually in the sample. Flexible ends are often cut off so that the protein will crystallise.

AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk) holds a predicted model for almost every UniProt protein. Search for the UniProt accession. The model is coloured by pLDDT, the confidence of the prediction for each residue (0–100):

pLDDT Meaning
above 90 very high: backbone and most side chains are reliable
70–90 confident: the backbone is probably right
50–70 low: treat with caution
below 50 very low: often a flexible, disordered region

On the UniProt entry, the Structure section lists both the PDB entries and the AlphaFold model, and the Disease & Variants section lists the diseases linked to the gene and the known disease-causing amino-acid changes (“Natural variant”, written like R187C: arginine 187 replaced by cysteine).

ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) collects how clinical laboratories classify each human variant (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign). Search for a gene with AQP2[gene], or for one variant by its protein change, for example AQP2 R187C.

B1. Two genes, one disease

The collecting duct needs two proteins to answer ADH: the ADH receptor in the basolateral membrane and the water channel that is inserted in the apical membrane. Search UniProtKB (recap from A1) for reviewed human entries that mention the disease:

"nephrogenic diabetes insipidus" AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true

Open each hit and read its Disease & Variants section.

  1. Which two genes are returned?
  2. Which of the two genes causes the X-linked form of the disease (NDI1)?
  3. In the Subcellular location section of the receptor entry, the Features table lists the membrane topology. How many “Transmembrane” segments are listed? Which large receptor family does this number suggest?
  4. Search ClinVar for AQP2 R187C and open the variant NM_000486.6(AQP2):c.559C>T (p.Arg187Cys). What is its germline classification?
Answer
  1. AVPR2 (P30518, vasopressin V2 receptor) and AQP2 (P41181, aquaporin-2).
  2. AVPR2. Most patients are boys, because males have only one X chromosome and so one faulty copy is enough. AQP2 causes the rarer autosomal form (NDI2).
  3. Seven. Seven transmembrane helices are typical of a G-protein-coupled receptor; the V2 receptor activates Gs, cAMP and protein kinase A, which moves AQP2 to the apical membrane.
  4. Pathogenic (shown as “Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic” because several laboratories have submitted it). In the Disease & Variants section of P41181 the same change is described as misfolded and without water-channel activity.

B2. A crystal structure of the water channel

Search the RCSB PDB for P41181 and open entry 4NEF, “X-ray structure of human Aquaporin 2”. Direct link: https://www.rcsb.org/structure/4NEF.

  1. Which experimental method was used, and what is the resolution (to the nearest 0.1 Å)?
  2. How many aquaporin-2 chains are in the deposited model? What does this tell you about the working form of the channel?

The UniProt entry lists the amino-acid changes found in NDI2 patients. B5. What a broken channel costs in litres

Question 6.2 gave two points: 600 mOsm a day excreted at 1,200 or 100 mOsm kg⁻¹. Here R draws the whole curve and adds two clinical ideas. Free-water clearance is the volume of pure water that the kidney removes from the body per day on top of an isosmotic urine: \(C_{\rm H_2O}=V-U_{\rm osm}V/P_{\rm osm}\). It is positive when the urine is more dilute than plasma. Use plasma osmolality 290 mOsm kg⁻¹. The 600 mOsm day⁻¹ load and the extreme urine osmolalities are typical textbook values.

solute <- 600                        # mOsm excreted per day
p_osm  <- 290                        # plasma osmolality, mOsm/kg
u_osm  <- seq(50, 1200, by = 10)     # urine osmolality, mOsm/kg
V <- solute / u_osm                  # urine volume, L/day
plot(u_osm, V, type = "l", log = "y",
     xlab = "Urine osmolality (mOsm/kg)", ylab = "Urine volume (L/day)")
abline(v = p_osm, lty = 2)
V[u_osm == 1200]; V[u_osm == 100]
free_water <- function(V, u_osm) V - u_osm * V / p_osm
round(free_water(0.5, 1200), 2)
round(free_water(6, 100), 2)
solute_ndi <- c(normal_diet = 600, low_salt_low_protein = 300)
solute_ndi / 100
