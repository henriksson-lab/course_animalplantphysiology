A4 — Water, ions and excretion

Why does living in water not solve water balance?

A freshwater fish is surrounded by water yet must control water entry and replace ions. A marine bony fish faces the opposite tendencies: it loses water and gains salts. A terrestrial mammal can conserve water, but eliminating nitrogen and dissipating heat still create costs. This unit connects the physical tendencies of water and solutes with epithelial transport, excretory organs and whole-animal regulation.

You should become able to predict osmotic tendencies, distinguish osmotic concentration from tonicity, compare aquatic strategies, trace a nephron, calculate excretion from filtration/reabsorption/secretion, and explain how water and nitrogen balance interact. Use the slides, exercises and quiz.

Begin with a boundary and a permeability

Water moves across a selectively permeable membrane in response to differences in water chemical potential. In an elementary osmotic comparison at equal pressure, the side containing more effectively retained solute tends to draw water from the other side. State which boundary is being considered: the skin, gill, intestinal wall, capillary wall and cell membrane are not interchangeable. Water entry into the animal and water entry into one of its cells are different questions.

Osmolarity counts osmotically active particles per litre of solution, often in osmoles L⁻¹ or milliosmoles L⁻¹. For an ideal teaching example, 0.10 mol L⁻¹ glucose contributes about 0.10 Osm L⁻¹, while 0.10 mol L⁻¹ NaCl contributes about 0.20 Osm L⁻¹ if treated as fully dissociated. Real solutions need not behave ideally. These particle counts are useful because osmosis depends on particle concentration, not simply the mass of dissolved material.

Tonicity predicts sustained effects on cell volume and depends on solutes that do not readily equilibrate across the relevant membrane. Two solutions with equal total osmolarity need not have the same effect if their solutes cross a membrane differently. Avoid treating every dissolved molecule as a permanently trapped osmotic particle. Hydrostatic pressure also matters: movement need not continue until solute concentrations are equal if an opposing pressure develops. The supporting reading introduces osmotic balance.

A permeable surface creates a transport problem

Respiratory surfaces must exchange gases, but permeability creates opportunities for water and ions to move as well. An animal cannot always make its surface perfectly impermeable while preserving other functions. It can instead alter surface properties, drink or stop drinking, move ions actively, and change urine volume and composition.

A typical freshwater teleost has body fluids more concentrated than the surrounding water. Water tends to enter and ions tend to be lost. It produces relatively large volumes of dilute urine and obtains ions through specialised transport processes and food. A typical marine teleost has body fluids less concentrated than seawater. It drinks seawater, absorbs water through the gut and eliminates excess salts through gill and renal processes. The two fish therefore use related epithelia to address different net challenges.

These are broad teleost examples, not descriptions of every fish. Marine elasmobranchs, such as sharks, retain organic osmolytes including urea and have a different strategy. Many marine invertebrates have total internal osmotic concentrations near those of their environment, yet may still regulate particular ions. An osmoconformer need not have every internal solute identical to seawater, and an osmoregulator need not maintain an absolutely unchanging internal concentration at every salinity. State the species and conditions before generalising.

Epithelia can move material against a tendency

An epithelial transport cell has different membrane domains facing different fluids. ATP-driven pumps establish ion gradients. Other proteins couple a solute’s movement to those gradients; channels allow selected ions to move down electrochemical gradients. Water can follow osmotic gradients through membranes and water channels. The epithelium and its cell junctions determine which routes are available.

This polarity makes directional transport possible. A list of pumps without their locations does not explain net movement from the environment to blood. Draw both boundaries of a cell and show how each protein contributes to an overall route. Energy expenditure can occur at one step while another step is passive. “Water is actively pumped by the kidney” is therefore a poor description of water following gradients produced by solute transport.

The nephron separates filtration from selective recovery

In a mammalian kidney, blood enters a glomerular capillary network. Filtration produces fluid in the nephron that normally contains water and many small solutes but few cells and little large plasma protein. A filtered molecule has left the blood and entered the tubular lumen; it has not necessarily left the body. Much of that filtered material is subsequently returned to blood.

Reabsorption moves material from tubular fluid towards blood. Secretion moves material from blood/interstitium towards tubular fluid. Excretion is the amount finally leaving in urine. For a stated substance and time interval, use the accounting relation:

\[\text{excretion}=\text{filtered load}-\text{reabsorption}+\text{secretion}.\]

For example, a filtered load of 100 arbitrary amount units min⁻¹, reabsorption of 98 and secretion of 1 gives excretion of 3 units min⁻¹. Keep amount per time distinct from concentration. If urine concentration is 2 mmol L⁻¹ and urine flow is 0.001 L min⁻¹, excretion is 0.002 mmol min⁻¹. Concentrated urine does not alone establish a large total amount excreted.

How can urine become more concentrated than plasma?

Water reabsorption requires an osmotic driving force and a water-permeable route. In the mammalian kidney, specialised loop segments differ in water permeability and solute transport. Their arrangement, together with other transport and urea-handling processes, helps establish a medullary osmotic gradient. Blood vessels serving the medulla help preserve this organisation while supplying tissue. The collecting duct passes through that gradient.

Antidiuretic hormone, ADH or vasopressin, can increase collecting-duct water permeability. More water then leaves the tubular fluid when an appropriate gradient exists, reducing urine volume and increasing its concentration. The hormone does not manufacture water or simply “make the kidney filter less”. The full concentrating mechanism is more complex than one salt pump or one hairpin loop. Here, be able to explain differential permeability, gradient, water recovery and hormonal control before memorising segment names.

When osmotic concentration of body fluids rises, sensing and control processes can increase ADH release and promote drinking. When the disturbance is corrected, feedback reduces the response. Blood-volume/pressure signals can also influence water conservation; it is not a one-input switch. Compare this loop with A1’s temperature example. The reading on hormonal regulation provides a complementary account.

Nitrogen disposal is part of the water budget

Breaking down amino acids creates nitrogen-containing waste. Ammonia can be released with relatively little conversion, but its handling requires attention to toxicity, concentration and removal. Urea costs energy to synthesise but can be retained or excreted at concentrations suitable for many animals. Uric acid can be eliminated with little water in a semi-solid form, though its production also has costs. No product is universally best: water availability, life stage, habitat and other physiological demands matter.

Terrestrial insects use Malpighian tubules and hindgut processes rather than mammalian nephrons. Many annelids and other invertebrates use different tubular excretory arrangements. Compare the tasks—removing waste, controlling ions, recovering water—before comparing anatomical names. Egestion of undigested food remains different from excretion of metabolically produced nitrogen.

Read structure and behaviour together

A kidney section may contain round tubule profiles, glomeruli and blood vessels, but section angle can change apparent shape. Look for a capillary tuft within a surrounding space when identifying a renal corpuscle; look for epithelial organisation around a lumen in tubules. These descriptions are preparation for the real slide set, not proof that a particular specimen is available. Finally, connect anatomy to the environment: a water-saving animal may also change behaviour, diet, ventilation and heat exchange. Continue to integration, movement and reproduction.
