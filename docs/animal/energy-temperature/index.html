A2 — Nutrition, energy and temperature

What does an animal spend its food on?

Two animals can eat equal masses of food yet differ in growth, activity and heat production. Food mass alone does not tell us how much energy was absorbed, which essential nutrients were obtained, or how that energy was used. This unit connects digestion to cellular metabolism and whole-animal energy balance, then asks how temperature changes the demands and limits of that system.

You should become able to distinguish ingestion, digestion and absorption; explain digestive specialisation; interpret a simple energy budget and oxygen consumption rate; distinguish aerobic and anaerobic ATP supply; and compare thermal strategies without equating ectothermy with inactivity. Use the slides, exercises and quiz.

From food to an absorbed molecule

Ingestion brings food into the digestive system. Mechanical processing changes particle size and mixes contents. Chemical digestion uses reactions, often enzyme-catalysed hydrolysis, to make smaller molecules available for absorption. Enzymes have particular substrates and working conditions; a digestive tract contains regions with different secretions and environments. The gut lumen is continuous with the outside world. A nutrient reaches the internal environment only after crossing an absorptive barrier.

In a mammalian small intestine, folds, villi and microscopic projections on epithelial cells increase the area available for absorption. Capillaries and lymphatic routes remove absorbed materials, helping maintain transport. Carbohydrate-derived monosaccharides and amino acids commonly enter blood, while much absorbed dietary lipid is packaged and enters lymph first. These routes show why “absorption” is not one identical membrane process for every nutrient. The organs supplying secretions also matter: pancreatic enzymes and bile contribute to processing food in the intestine.

Eliminating unabsorbed material in faeces is egestion. Removing metabolic wastes produced within the animal is excretion. The distinction matters when constructing an energy budget or explaining a kidney’s function. OpenStax offers a supporting account of digestive processes.

Digestive form follows the food problem

Animal diets differ in nutrient concentration, physical structure and chemical defences. A predatory animal faces different processing demands from an animal eating fibrous plant tissue. Many animals lack enzymes that directly digest cellulose effectively. Symbiotic microbes can break down plant polymers and produce compounds the host can use. The host must provide conditions and retention time that sustain that fermentation.

In a ruminant, microbial fermentation occurs before the small intestine. Microbes and their products can then contribute to the host’s nutrition. Hindgut fermenters maintain substantial fermentation later in the tract. Where fermentation occurs changes which products are readily absorbed and whether microbial biomass reaches major sites of protein digestion. These strategies involve trade-offs in passage time, food intake and nutrient recovery; one is not universally superior for every food supply or body size.

For histology, relate an absorptive epithelium to its lumen and underlying transport tissues. Smooth muscle layers help propel contents, while glands and specialised epithelial cells contribute secretions. A diagram that labels “villus” without showing the lumen, epithelial barrier and internal removal route misses the functional explanation. The broad comparative anatomy is covered in the reading on digestive systems.

Keep energy accounting explicit

For a simplified animal over a stated interval, ingested energy is divided between material not absorbed and absorbed energy. After accounting for energy leaving in excreted products, the remaining energy can support metabolic expenditure and net storage or production. Exact ecological bookkeeping conventions vary, so define your terms before calculating. Here we use:

\[ E_{\rm ingested}=E_{\rm faeces}+E_{\rm excreted}+E_{\rm metabolic}+E_{\rm retained}. \]

All quantities refer to the same interval. If intake is 100 kJ day⁻¹, faecal loss 25, excreted chemical energy 5 and metabolic expenditure 60 kJ day⁻¹, retained energy is 10 kJ day⁻¹. Retained energy can contribute to new tissue, reserves or reproductive products. A negative retained balance can mean the animal is drawing down stores. This arithmetic does not identify which nutrient limited growth, because energy and essential nutrient supplies are distinct.

Avoid double counting. Internal mechanical work ultimately dissipates largely as heat, so a budget that already includes it in metabolic expenditure should not add the same energy again as an independent heat cost. Also distinguish energy in joules from power, an energy rate in watts. One watt is one joule per second. A daily intake and an instantaneous metabolic power cannot be subtracted until converted to compatible units and time intervals.

ATP supply, work and oxygen consumption

ATP links fuel metabolism to processes such as transport and muscle activity. Aerobic metabolism uses oxygen in mitochondrial electron transfer and can support sustained ATP supply when fuels, oxygen delivery and waste removal are adequate. Anaerobic pathways can supply ATP without that immediate oxygen use, but their ATP yield per unit substrate and sustainable rates differ. An animal may use both during a single bout of activity. Oxygen consumption therefore provides useful information about aerobic metabolism without being a complete record of every ATP-producing process at every instant.

Imagine a supplied respirometry observation: a chamber contains 0.10 L of water, and oxygen concentration falls by 0.40 mg L⁻¹ in 10 minutes. The chamber’s oxygen loss is \(0.10\times0.40=0.040\) mg, giving an apparent rate of 0.0040 mg min⁻¹. If a comparable empty chamber loses 0.0010 mg min⁻¹, the blank-corrected animal rate is 0.0030 mg min⁻¹. The example is a calculation with supplied data, not a procedure for keeping animals in sealed containers.

Rate comparisons require the right denominator and context. Dividing by body mass produces a mass-specific rate, but does not automatically remove all effects of body size. Activity, temperature, feeding state, stress and time of day can also matter. Repeated readings from one animal do not create new independent animals. Any actual experiment must use the established approved protocol and conditions; physiological interpretation begins by understanding what the measurement system measures.

Temperature changes both rates and costs

An ectotherm obtains much of the heat determining its body temperature from external sources. An endotherm generates substantial metabolic heat for temperature regulation. These terms concern heat sources. They are not identical to poikilothermy and homeothermy, which describe variation or relative stability of body temperature. An ectotherm can regulate temperature behaviourally, and an endotherm may allow body temperature to vary.

Temperature affects reaction rates, membranes and protein function. A rise can increase some physiological rates within a suitable range, yet eventually performance declines as other processes become limiting or damaged. A simple \(Q_{10}\) describes the rate ratio over a 10 °C interval:

\[Q_{10}=\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^{10/(T_2-T_1)}.\]

If a rate increases from 2 to 4 units between 10 and 20 °C, \(Q_{10}=2\). It is a description over those conditions, not a universal constant or a license to extrapolate into thermal extremes. Use rates measured under comparable feeding, activity and acclimation conditions.

Endotherms face an energy cost when heat loss must be replaced. Insulation, posture, peripheral blood flow and behaviour alter heat exchange. Below a species- and condition-dependent thermoneutral range, resting metabolic heat production often rises as ambient temperature falls. Above a suitable range, cooling can become demanding; evaporation loses water. An ectotherm’s metabolic rate may rise with environmental warming over a moderate range. Thus two rising metabolic rates can reflect different mechanisms: defending body temperature in cold conditions or temperature-dependent processes in a warming body.
