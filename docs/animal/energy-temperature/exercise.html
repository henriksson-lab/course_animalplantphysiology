A2 exercises — Feeding, metabolism and temperature

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Digestion and absorption

1.1

Define each term: ingestion, digestion, absorption, egestion and excretion.

Answer
  • Ingestion: taking food into the digestive tract.
  • Digestion: breaking food down, mechanically and chemically (mainly by enzymatic hydrolysis), into molecules small enough to be absorbed.
  • Absorption: uptake of the products of digestion across the gut epithelium into the internal environment (blood or lymph).
  • Egestion: elimination of unabsorbed material from the gut as faeces.
  • Excretion: elimination of metabolic wastes produced inside the body, such as urea.

1.2

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. A starch grain in the lumen of the small intestine is inside the body’s internal environment.
  2. Faeces are the main route for the excretion of nitrogenous waste in mammals.
  3. Bile is a digestive enzyme that hydrolyses fats.
  4. Chemical digestion of food is mostly by hydrolysis.
Answer
  1. False. The gut lumen is continuous with the outside world. Material is inside the internal environment only after it has crossed the epithelium.
  2. False. Faeces are mainly egested, unabsorbed material. Nitrogenous waste (urea) is excreted mainly in urine.
  3. False. Bile contains no digestive enzymes. Its bile salts emulsify fats into small droplets, which increases the surface area for lipase.
  4. True.

1.4

Put the steps in order for starch in a mammalian meal:

  1. glucose crosses the basolateral membrane of the enterocyte into the blood
  2. salivary amylase begins to hydrolyse starch in the mouth
  3. glucose is taken up across the apical membrane of the enterocyte
  4. pancreatic amylase hydrolyses starch in the small intestine
  5. brush-border enzymes (such as maltase) hydrolyse disaccharides to glucose
  6. blood from the intestine reaches the liver in the hepatic portal vein
Answer

b → d → e → c → a → f.

1.5

Name three structural features of the mammalian small intestine that increase its absorptive surface area, from the largest to the smallest scale.

Answer
  1. Circular folds of the intestinal wall.
  2. Villi: finger-like projections of the mucosa.
  3. Microvilli: projections of the apical membrane of each epithelial cell (the brush border).

1.6

State whether each absorbed product enters the blood capillaries or the lacteals (lymphatic vessels) of a villus.

  1. glucose
  2. amino acids
  3. long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides, repackaged as chylomicrons
Answer
  1. Blood capillaries. b. Blood capillaries. c. Lacteals. Chylomicrons are too large to enter the blood capillaries, so they enter the lymph and reach the blood later.

Microbial fermentation

2.1

Why do most herbivorous vertebrates depend on symbiotic microbes to use plant cell walls, and which main products do the microbes supply to the host?

Answer

Vertebrates do not produce cellulase, so they cannot hydrolyse cellulose themselves. Microbes ferment the cellulose and supply short-chain (volatile) fatty acids, such as acetate, propionate and butyrate, which the host absorbs and uses as fuel. The microbes themselves are also a source of protein.

2.2

Classify each animal as a foregut fermenter or a hindgut fermenter.

  1. cow
  2. horse
  3. sheep
  4. rabbit
Answer
  1. Foregut (ruminant). b. Hindgut (caecum and colon). c. Foregut (ruminant).
  2. Hindgut (caecum).

2.3

In which type of fermenter is microbial protein digested and absorbed in the small intestine, and why? How do rabbits recover microbial protein?

Answer

In a foregut fermenter. The fermentation chamber lies before the stomach and small intestine, so microbes washed out of it are digested in the small intestine like other food protein. In a hindgut fermenter the microbes grow after the small intestine and are mostly lost in the faeces. Rabbits recover them by caecotrophy: they eat the soft faeces produced from the caecum (coprophagy).

Energy budgets

3.1

Over one day, an animal has ingested energy 150 kJ, faecal loss 40 kJ, chemical energy excreted (in urine) 10 kJ and metabolic expenditure 85 kJ. Use the budget

\[ E_{\rm ingested}=E_{\rm faeces}+E_{\rm excreted}+E_{\rm metabolic}+E_{\rm retained}. \]

  1. Calculate the retained energy.
  2. Calculate the absorbed energy as a percentage of ingested energy.
  3. Calculate the retained energy if metabolic expenditure rises to 110 kJ, with the other terms unchanged. What does the sign of the result mean?
Answer
  1. \(150-40-10-85=15\) kJ.
  2. Absorbed energy is \(150-40=110\) kJ, i.e. \(110/150=73\,\%\) of ingested energy.
  3. \(150-40-10-110=-10\) kJ. The animal spends more than it takes up, so it draws on its energy stores (such as fat or glycogen) and loses body energy.

3.2

Express the metabolic expenditure of 85 kJ day⁻¹ from 3.1 as a mean power in watts. (1 day = 86 400 s.)

Answer

\(85\,000\ \text{J} / 86\,400\ \text{s} = 0.98\) J s⁻¹ \(= 0.98\) W.

3.3

Which quantity is energy and which is power? Give the SI unit of each.

  1. 2 000 kJ of food eaten in a day
  2. a resting metabolic rate of 80 W
  3. 60 J transferred in 10 s
Answer
  1. Energy (J). b. Power (W = J s⁻¹). c. Energy (J); it corresponds to a mean power of \(60/10=6\) W.

4.2

Why does an animal’s oxygen consumption during a sprint underestimate its total rate of ATP use?

Answer

During a sprint, part of the ATP comes from anaerobic sources (phosphocreatine and glycolysis to lactate), which do not consume O₂. Oxygen consumption measures only aerobic ATP production.

Temperature and metabolism

5.1

Use \(Q_{10}=(R_2/R_1)^{10/(T_2-T_1)}\).

  1. A rate rises from 2.0 to 8.0 units between 10 and 30 °C. Calculate \(Q_{10}\).
  2. A process has \(Q_{10}=2.5\) and a rate of 4.0 units at 20 °C. Predict the rate at 30 °C.
Answer
  1. \(Q_{10}=(8.0/2.0)^{10/20}=4^{0.5}=2.0\).
  2. \(4.0\times2.5=10\) units.

5.2

Match each term to its definition: ectotherm, endotherm, poikilotherm, homeotherm.

  1. an animal whose body temperature varies with environmental temperature
  2. an animal whose body temperature is determined mainly by external heat sources
  3. an animal that keeps a relatively constant body temperature
  4. an animal that warms its body mainly with its own metabolic heat
Answer
  1. Poikilotherm. b. Ectotherm. c. Homeotherm. d. Endotherm.

Ectothermy and endothermy refer to the source of body heat; poikilothermy and homeothermy refer to how much body temperature varies.

5.3

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Ectotherms cannot regulate their body temperature.
  2. In a resting ectotherm, metabolic rate usually rises when environmental temperature rises over a moderate range.
  3. In a resting endotherm, metabolic rate is lowest within the thermoneutral zone.
  4. A measured \(Q_{10}\) can be used to predict rates at any temperature.
Answer
  1. False. Many ectotherms regulate body temperature by behaviour, such as basking, seeking shade and changing posture.
  2. True. Body temperature follows environmental temperature, and reaction rates rise with temperature.
  3. True.
  4. False. \(Q_{10}\) describes only the measured temperature range. Outside it, rates level off and fall as proteins and membranes are damaged.

5.4

The graph of resting metabolic rate against ambient temperature for a mammal has three regions. Name the region in which metabolic rate is at its minimum and constant, and the temperature at its lower boundary. What happens to metabolic rate below that boundary, and why?

Answer

The thermoneutral zone; its lower boundary is the lower critical temperature. Below the lower critical temperature, metabolic rate rises as ambient temperature falls, because the animal must produce more heat (by shivering and non-shivering thermogenesis) to replace the faster heat loss and keep body temperature constant.

5.5

Name the route of heat exchange in each example: conduction, convection, radiation or evaporation.

  1. A lizard warms itself in sunlight.
  2. A dog pants on a hot day.
  3. A snake lies on a warm rock.
  4. Wind cools a bird’s body surface.
Answer
  1. Radiation. b. Evaporation. c. Conduction. d. Convection.

5.6

Name four ways in which a mammal reduces heat loss or increases heat production in the cold.

Answer

Any four of: thicker fur or fat (insulation); piloerection (raising the fur); vasoconstriction of skin blood vessels; countercurrent heat exchange in the limbs; curling up (posture) and huddling; shivering thermogenesis; non-shivering thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue.

Bioinformatics and R

Brown fat cells do something that looks wasteful: they burn fuel without making ATP, releasing the energy as heat. The key protein is uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), UniProt P25874. These exercises use three new resources to find out what UCP1 is, what it does and where it is made: InterPro/Pfam (protein domains and families), Gene Ontology (standard terms for function) and the Human Protein Atlas (tissue expression). UCP1 is compared with its relative, the ADP/ATP translocase ANT1 (P12235), which does an everyday job in every mitochondrion. The last two exercises use R on real body temperatures of wild beavers. Run the R code in R or RStudio with base R only; B1 reads sequences from the internet.

How to look up domains, GO terms and tissue expression

Find the protein’s UniProt accession first (unit A1), for example with gene:UCP1 AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true.

InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) collects protein signatures from several member databases, of which Pfam is the best known. Open https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/protein/UniProt/<accession>/. The graphic shows the protein as a line with coloured bars: each bar is a match, labelled by its type: family (the whole protein belongs to a group), domain (a structural unit that can occur in different proteins), repeat (a short unit occurring several times) or homologous superfamily. Pfam accessions start with PF, InterPro accessions with IPR. The Family & Domains section of a UniProt entry shows the same information more briefly.

Gene Ontology (GO) describes function with standard terms in three aspects: molecular function (what the protein does as a molecule), biological process (the larger process it contributes to) and cellular component (where it is). Every term has an ID such as GO:0009409. The UniProt entry lists a protein’s GO terms in the Function section (molecular function and biological process) and in Subcellular Location (cellular component); QuickGO (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/QuickGO/) shows the definition of each term.

Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org) measures RNA and protein in human tissues and cells. Search the gene name and open the Tissue page. RNA levels are given as nTPM (normalised transcripts per million); the bar chart shows which tissues have the most. The Single cell type page shows which cell types express the gene.

B1. Two relatives with different jobs: domains

UCP1 and ANT1 both sit in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Open https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/protein/UniProt/P25874/ (UCP1) and https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/protein/UniProt/P12235/ (ANT1).

  1. Which Pfam entry (accession and name) matches UCP1, and is it of type domain, repeat or family?
  2. How many times does it occur in UCP1, and how many times in ANT1?

B2. Same family, different function: GO terms

Open the Function sections of https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/P25874/entry (UCP1) and https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/P12235/entry (ANT1) and look at their GO annotations. Use QuickGO for definitions.

  1. Which cellular-component term do both proteins share that tells you which membrane they work in?
  2. Which of these biological-process terms is annotated to UCP1 but not to ANT1?
    1. adaptive thermogenesis (GO:1990845)
    2. response to cold (GO:0009409)
    3. mitochondrial ATP transmembrane transport (GO:1990544)
    4. ADP transport (GO:0015866)
  3. Which molecular-function term describes ANT1’s everyday job of exchanging nucleotides? Give its GO ID.
Answer
  1. Mitochondrial inner membrane (GO:0005743), where the proton gradient made by the respiratory chain drives ATP synthase.
  2. 2, response to cold. Options 3 and 4 belong to ANT1. Option 1 is annotated to both: ANT1 also lets some protons leak back, so it adds a little heat in all cells. GO terms describe evidence-backed roles, and one protein can have several.
  3. ATP:ADP antiporter activity, GO:0005471. ANT1 exports each new ATP from the mitochondrion in exchange for an ADP. UCP1 instead lets protons return to the matrix without passing ATP synthase, so the energy of the gradient becomes heat.

B3. Where are the two genes switched on?

On the Human Protein Atlas, open the tissue pages https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000109424-UCP1/tissue and https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000151729-SLC25A4/tissue (SLC25A4 is the gene for ANT1).

  1. Which tissue has the highest UCP1 RNA in the tissue bar chart?
  2. On UCP1’s Single cell type page, which cell type has the highest expression?
  3. The three tissues with the highest SLC25A4 RNA are heart muscle, skeletal muscle and tongue. Which of the four tissue categories (unit A1) do they share, and why does that fit ANT1’s job?
Answer
  1. Adrenal gland, and at a low level; most other tissues show none. The HPA “adipose tissue” samples are subcutaneous white fat, which has little UCP1. In adult humans, brown and beige fat lie in particular depots, for example around the kidneys and adrenal glands and in the neck, so an adrenal sample can include some of it.
  2. Adipocytes (fat cells).
  3. Muscle tissue. Contracting muscle uses ATP at a high rate, so its mitochondria must export a lot of ATP. UCP1 is restricted to a few specialised fat cells whose job is heat, whereas ANT1 is abundant wherever ATP demand is high.

B4. Does a beaver warm up when it goes out?

B5. Warming up before leaving: how much does it cost?

The plot from B4 shows that the beaver’s temperature already rises while it is still resting in the lodge. Fit a line to the inside readings to get the warming rate, then estimate the heat stored. The body mass (20 kg), the specific heat of tissue (3.5 kJ kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹) and the energy of fat (39 kJ g⁻¹) are assumed round values, not part of the dataset.

inside <- b[b$activ == 0, ]                   # needs b from B4
fit <- lm(temp ~ hour, data = inside)
round(coef(fit), 3)

  1. 0.078 °C per hour (about 0.5 °C over the 6 h inside).
  2. 33.5 kJ, about 0.86 g of fat. Warming the body itself is cheap; the larger cost is replacing the heat lost to cold water once outside.
  3. About 1.5 W, on top of the power needed to balance ongoing heat loss.
  4. Non-shivering thermogenesis in brown fat, with UCP1. These data show that heat production rose before the beaver moved off; they cannot show which tissue produced it. A beaver’s brown fat would make heat exactly as in B2: protons flow back through UCP1, fuel is oxidised without making ATP, and the energy appears as heat.
