Revision and integration

These are formative study tasks, not an official examination or a prediction of its questions. Attempt each explanation before opening the feedback. Include the causal chain: structure or signal → process → physiological consequence.

Animal half

1. From tissue to oxygen demand

Compare a thin exchange epithelium with a thick stratified protective epithelium. Explain why their structures suit different functions. Then trace how an increase in muscle oxygen demand can be met through ventilation, exchange and circulation. Name one observation you would need before classifying a real tissue section.

Feedback

A short diffusion distance favours exchange; multiple layers can protect against abrasion. Neither alone identifies an organ. Cell shape, layering, lumen, extracellular material and neighbouring tissues provide evidence. Ventilation renews the respiratory medium, diffusion crosses the exchange barrier, blood flow distributes oxygen, and tissues extract it. Increased demand need not be met by changing just one step. Microscopy interpretation must use the actual image and scale, not a guessed label from a written clue.

2. Energy and temperature

Two simulated animals consume 18 and 30 µmol oxygen over six minutes. Both have mass 0.5 g. Calculate the whole-animal and mass-specific mean rates. The animals differ in both temperature and feeding history. What comparison is possible, and what causal claim is unsupported?

Feedback

Whole-animal rates are 3 and 5 µmol min⁻¹. Mass-specific values are 6 and 10 µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹. The observed rates differ, but temperature and feeding history are confounded; neither cause can be isolated. Oxygen consumption is an indicator of aerobic metabolism under the stated conditions, not a direct measure of all energy use or a fitness score.

3. Two osmotic environments

Compare the water-balance problems faced by a freshwater fish and a terrestrial mammal. Explain how exchange surfaces, ion transport and excretion contribute. Why does “water follows salt” fail as a complete explanation of kidney function?

Feedback

A typical freshwater teleost gains water osmotically and loses ions across permeable surfaces; dilute urine and active ion uptake help maintain balance. A terrestrial mammal loses water to the environment and must balance intake, metabolic production and losses. Kidney filtration, selective reabsorption, secretion, medullary gradients and regulated collecting-duct permeability all matter. Water movement depends on a pathway as well as a driving gradient. There are diverse exceptions to broad group descriptions.

4. A signal to movement

Explain how a neural signal can produce skeletal-muscle force, and how endocrine signals can influence the resources available for activity. Distinguish changes in action-potential frequency from changes in action-potential amplitude.

Feedback

In a vertebrate example, a motor neuron activates the neuromuscular junction, muscle membrane excitation triggers calcium release, and calcium permits actin–myosin interaction. ATP supports cross-bridge cycling and calcium handling. Recruitment and activation frequency contribute to force regulation. Hormones can alter fuel mobilisation and longer-term tissue properties. A stronger stimulus generally changes firing/recruitment rather than scaling each regenerative action potential as if it were an analogue voltage signal.

Plant half

5. Water and carbon together

Explain how dry air can increase the demand for water loss, why stomatal closure can reduce that loss, and how closure can constrain carbon assimilation. Does this imply every leaf under drought has the same photosynthetic rate?

Feedback

Water-vapour loss depends on the vapour gradient and pathway conductance. Closing stomata lowers conductance but also restricts CO₂ entry. Leaf temperature, boundary layer, soil/root hydraulic supply, biochemistry, species and acclimation also matter. Describe a trade-off and its conditions, not a universal numerical response. Stomata are regulated pores, rather than actively pumping water vapour.

6. Net exchange is a balance

A teaching model gives gross fixation of 14 and respiratory loss of 3 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ under one light condition. Calculate net uptake. In a second condition net uptake is zero. Does zero net uptake show that all metabolism has stopped? What extra assumption would be needed to estimate gross fixation from a dark-respiration measurement?

Feedback

Net uptake is 11 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ under the supplied simplified balance. Zero net uptake means opposing fluxes balance; processes can remain active. Using a dark rate to correct a light measurement assumes the relevant respiratory loss is suitably represented under illumination. Real measurements can also involve photorespiration and condition-dependent fluxes. State what the teaching model includes before interpreting it as a full carbon budget.

7. A developmental response

Propose how to distinguish failure of seed germination from failure to supply permissive conditions. Include viability, water, temperature, light where relevant, and dormancy. Explain why a hormone response depends on tissue and stage.

Feedback

Use a suitable viable seed lot, record environmental conditions, include an appropriate reference and define germination before counting. Non-germination can reflect dead seeds, unsuitable conditions or dormancy; the observation alone does not distinguish them. Hormone responses depend on receptors, downstream machinery, sensitivity, other signals and developmental competence. Different tissues need not respond identically to the same external dose.

8. An application claim

A line of modified plants has greater dry mass than its comparison line in one controlled trial. Draft a conclusion about the observation, then list evidence needed before recommending deployment under field drought.

Feedback

The observed dry-mass difference applies to the measured lines and conditions. Check independent allocation, genetic-background controls, replication, measurement timing and water use. Field performance, yield, environmental interactions, non-target effects, management, access and stakeholder priorities need evidence. Neither the modification method alone nor greater biomass in one setting determines the whole benefit–risk judgement.

Integrating both halves

9. Transport is gradient plus pathway

Make a comparison table for a gill, a kidney epithelial pathway and a root-to-leaf water pathway. For each identify the transported substance, driving force, barrier/pathway, regulatory process and a cost or limitation. Do not assume that all three use the same mechanism because all transport material.

Feedback

Gill oxygen crosses along a partial-pressure gradient; ventilation and blood flow help maintain it. Kidney epithelia combine selective channels/transporters with ion gradients and regulated water permeability. Root-to-leaf water flow depends on water-potential differences and hydraulic pathways, with evaporation and stomatal regulation important in transpiring plants. Diffusion distance, energy for gradient maintenance and hydraulic resistance impose different constraints. Distinguish bulk flow from membrane transport.

10. Evidence before an explanation

Choose one supplied practical dataset. Write five sentences: the question; the independent unit and comparison; a result with units; a mechanism consistent with that result; and a limitation or alternative explanation. Draw the result without using R. Swap explanations with a partner and identify any sentence that states more than the design supports.

Feedback criteria

A complete response separates observation from mechanism and mechanism from proof. The calculation and figure must match the data dictionary. An explanation should be consistent with the unit tutorial, while the limitation should name a specific design/measurement issue. No particular software or p-value is required to achieve this.
