Start here: how to study physiology

Work from a question to a mechanism

A fish moves water across its gills; a leaf loses water through stomata. Both have an exchange surface, a gradient and a transport pathway, but the structures and regulatory mechanisms differ. Learning physiology means explaining those connections. Naming a part is the start of an explanation.

For each unit, read the tutorial, use the short slides to revisit the main connections, attempt the exercises and then try the self-check quiz. Explain your answers before opening a solution. A correct choice with an incorrect mechanism needs another attempt.

Use the ten-week route to organise work and the glossary to recover terminology. Unit reading links are provided for context; the course’s adopted textbook and page assignments still require confirmation. You do not need to buy the candidate textbooks to use these original tutorials.

A small prerequisite bridge

Compartments and membranes

A cell membrane separates aqueous compartments. Its lipid bilayer restricts many polar and charged substances, while specific proteins provide pathways. An ion channel permits movement down an electrochemical gradient when open. A transporter binds a substance and changes conformation; some transporters couple movement to an energy source. A concentration difference alone does not fully predict an ion’s movement because voltage also matters.

Diffusion moves material down a chemical-potential gradient without requiring the cell to expend ATP at that step. ATP-consuming pumps can establish the gradients that later power other movements. Thus passive movement and active gradient maintenance can be parts of the same physiological system.

Explain it: why can a cell require ATP while glucose enters by a transporter that does not itself split ATP? Identify both the immediate transport step and the machinery that maintains its driving gradient.

Check your explanation

In a sodium–glucose cotransport example, glucose entry is coupled to sodium moving down its electrochemical gradient. A sodium/potassium pump elsewhere in the membrane uses ATP to maintain the sodium gradient. The coupled uptake depends indirectly on that ATP expenditure.

Enzymes, energy and matter

Enzymes speed reactions; they do not create energy or change the overall equilibrium of a reaction. Metabolic pathways couple energetically favourable and unfavourable processes. ATP is an energy-transfer molecule, while carbon compounds can supply both building material and chemical energy. Trace carbon atoms separately from energy transfer when studying photosynthesis or respiration.

An animal obtains organic carbon from food. A green plant can incorporate inorganic carbon into organic molecules using photosynthetic energy input. Both require respiration and regulated transport. A plant root does not normally obtain its carbon supply by photosynthesising underground.

Genes, signals and development

A gene is a DNA sequence whose expression contributes a functional product, such as an RNA or a protein. Expression can differ between tissues even when their cells contain broadly the same nuclear genome. A signal can alter protein activity quickly or alter gene expression over a longer period. A cell’s response also depends on its receptors, developmental state and other signals.

Organismal development involves regulated division, expansion, differentiation and cell death. More cells and larger cells are different mechanisms of growth. Having a gene does not guarantee that every tissue expresses it at the same level or produces the same phenotype in every environment.

Feedback and adaptation

In negative feedback, the response opposes a deviation in a regulated variable. The variable still fluctuates; a steady average does not mean nothing is happening. Positive feedback reinforces a change and often requires an external ending event. Neither label tells you whether a process is beneficial.

An individual can acclimate during its lifetime. Evolutionary adaptation concerns heritable differences across generations in a population. A single before–after experiment cannot demonstrate evolutionary adaptation.

Read quantities before calculating

Write down the measured quantity, its unit, the direction of a difference and what one observation represents. Then check one small example by hand.

Expression Meaning Example
Final − initial Signed change 4.6 g − 5.0 g = −0.4 g
Change / time Average rate over an interval 12 µmol / 4 min = 3 µmol min⁻¹
100 × change / initial Relative change in percent −0.4 / 5.0 × 100 = −8%
Quantity / mass Value expressed per unit mass 3 µmol min⁻¹ / 0.5 g = 6 µmol min⁻¹ g⁻¹

A negative mass change describes loss under this definition. Water-loss magnitude would instead be initial minus final. Always state the convention. A value per gram is not proof that all body-size effects have been removed.

Working with practical data

The supplied CSVs are simulated teaching data. They support checking calculations, plotting observations and reasoning about design. They are not observations of actual organisms or evidence for a real treatment effect. The data dictionary states what each row means and how the data were constructed.

Use paper, a calculator or an established supported analysis method for the core activities. Optional R support repeats one analysis for students and teachers who choose it; core learning does not depend on R.

Keep the original measurements. Explain an unusual value using records and experimental context before changing or excluding it. Repeated readings from one animal, pot or chamber do not automatically create independent replicates.

What is ready, and what your teacher supplies

The tutorials, slides, exercises, quizzes and classroom analysis activities form a first teaching draft. Live specimen sessions need the teacher’s actual images, specimens and laboratory protocols. The official timetable, adopted readings, compulsory elements and assessment arrangements come from the teaching team. The classroom activities here do not replace hands-on anatomy or experimentation.
