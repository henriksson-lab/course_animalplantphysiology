P2 exercises — Water, minerals and transport

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Water potential

1.1

Define water potential (Ψ), state its usual unit in plant physiology, and give the water potential of pure water at atmospheric pressure.

Answer

Water potential is the chemical potential (free energy per unit volume) of water relative to that of pure water at atmospheric pressure and the same temperature. It is expressed in megapascals (MPa). Pure water at atmospheric pressure has Ψ = 0 MPa.

1.2

Arrange from the highest to the lowest water potential: −0.8 MPa, 0 MPa, −1.5 MPa, −0.2 MPa. In which direction does water move passively?

Answer

0 > −0.2 > −0.8 > −1.5 MPa. Water moves passively from higher (less negative) to lower (more negative) water potential.

1.3

Use Ψ = Ψs + Ψp (ignore gravity and matric potential). Both cells are placed in the same external solution with Ψ = −0.4 MPa.

Cell Ψs (MPa) Ψp (MPa) Ψ (MPa) Initial net water movement
A −0.8 +0.2
B −0.7 +0.5
Answer
Cell Ψs (MPa) Ψp (MPa) Ψ (MPa) Initial net water movement
A −0.8 +0.2 −0.6 Into the cell (−0.4 > −0.6)
B −0.7 +0.5 −0.2 Out of the cell (−0.2 > −0.4)

Cell B loses water even though its solute potential is more negative than the external solution, because total Ψ determines direction.

1.4

Calculate the solute potential at 25 °C (298 K) of

  1. 0.1 mol L⁻¹ sucrose
  2. 0.1 mol L⁻¹ NaCl (assume complete dissociation)

Use Ψs = −cRT, where c is the osmolar concentration and R = 0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Answer
  1. Ψs = −(0.1 mol L⁻¹)(0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(298 K) = −0.25 MPa.
  2. NaCl gives two particles, so c = 0.2 osmol L⁻¹ and Ψs = −0.50 MPa.

1.5

A flaccid cell (Ψp = 0) has Ψs = −0.6 MPa. Assume the cell volume, and hence Ψs, does not change.

  1. What is its Ψ?
  2. It is placed in pure water. What are Ψ and Ψp at equilibrium?
  3. It is instead placed in a solution with Ψ = −0.8 MPa. In which direction does water move, and what is Ψp at the end?
Answer
  1. Ψ = −0.6 + 0 = −0.6 MPa.
  2. Water enters until Ψ = 0 MPa; then Ψp = 0 − (−0.6) = +0.6 MPa.
  3. Water leaves the cell (−0.6 > −0.8). Ψp stays at 0 (it cannot become strongly negative in a living cell); the protoplast shrinks away from the wall (plasmolysis).

1.6

A root cell has Ψ = −0.4 MPa. Give the initial direction of net water movement between the cell and the soil solution when the soil solution has

  1. Ψ = −0.1 MPa
  2. Ψ = −0.6 MPa (saline soil)
Answer
  1. From soil into the root cell.
  2. From the root cell into the soil. Saline soil can therefore cause water stress although it is wet.

Uptake by roots

2.1

Name the three routes by which water crosses the root cortex and state which structure each route passes through.

Answer
  • Apoplastic: through cell walls and intercellular spaces, without crossing membranes.
  • Symplastic: from cytoplasm to cytoplasm through plasmodesmata.
  • Transmembrane (transcellular): across the plasma membrane (and tonoplast) of each successive cell.

2.2

  1. In which cell layer is the Casparian strip found?
  2. Which compounds make it?
  3. What is its function?
Answer
  1. The endodermis, in the radial and transverse walls.
  2. Suberin (with lignin).
  3. It blocks the apoplastic route into the stele, so water and solutes must cross a plasma membrane, which allows selective uptake.

2.3

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Aquaporins pump water into root cells using ATP.
  2. The plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase uses ATP to build an electrochemical proton gradient.
  3. A proton-coupled carrier can move a nutrient ion against its own concentration gradient.
  4. Inhibiting the H⁺-ATPase directly stops passive water flow through aquaporins.
Answer
  1. False. Aquaporins are channels that increase membrane permeability to water; water moves passively down its potential gradient.
  2. True.
  3. True. Energy comes from protons moving down their electrochemical gradient (secondary active transport).
  4. False. It reduces proton-coupled solute uptake; water flow through aquaporins is passive and not directly driven by ATP.

2.4

Waterlogged soil contains plenty of water, yet mineral uptake by roots falls. Give the chain of causes.

Answer

Water fills the soil pores → little O₂ reaches the roots → aerobic respiration falls → less ATP → H⁺-ATPase activity and the proton gradient decline → less active (proton-coupled) uptake of mineral ions.

Xylem transport and transpiration

3.1

Arrange the steps of water movement in a transpiring plant in order:

  1. water vapour diffuses out through the stomatal pore
  2. water crosses the root endodermis
  3. water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls into the air spaces
  4. water enters the root from the soil
  5. water moves up the xylem under tension
Answer

d → b → e → c → a.

3.2

Match each term to its definition: cohesion, adhesion, tension, cavitation.

  1. Negative hydrostatic pressure in the xylem water
  2. Attraction between water molecules through hydrogen bonding
  3. Breakage of the water column and formation of a gas bubble in a conduit
  4. Attraction between water molecules and the conduit walls
Answer

Cohesion–2, adhesion–4, tension–1, cavitation–3. A gas-filled conduit after cavitation is said to be embolised.

3.3

Typical water potentials in a transpiring plant are listed. Arrange them in the order water passes and state what drives the flow.

Leaf cells: −0.8 MPa. Soil: −0.1 MPa. Atmosphere (50 % relative humidity): −95 MPa. Root xylem: −0.4 MPa.

Answer

Soil (−0.1) → root xylem (−0.4) → leaf cells (−0.8) → atmosphere (−95 MPa). Water moves passively down this gradient of decreasing water potential. The energy comes from evaporation at the leaf (ultimately solar energy); the plant does not pump water.

3.4

Four pots are weighed over 4 h. Unplanted soil controls lose 4 g per pot over the same time.

Pot Mass loss (g) Leaf area (m²)
1 24 0.10
2 27 0.12
3 22 0.10
4 25 0.11
  1. Calculate the transpiration rate of each pot in g m⁻² h⁻¹ after subtracting soil evaporation.
  2. Calculate the mean rate.
  3. Convert the rate of pot 1 to mmol H₂O m⁻² s⁻¹ (M(H₂O) = 18 g mol⁻¹).
Answer
  1. Rate = (loss − 4 g) / (4 h × leaf area).
Pot Corrected loss (g) Rate (g m⁻² h⁻¹)
1 20 50.0
2 23 47.9
3 18 45.0
4 21 47.7
  1. (50.0 + 47.9 + 45.0 + 47.7) / 4 = 47.7 g m⁻² h⁻¹.
  2. 50 g m⁻² h⁻¹ ÷ 18 g mol⁻¹ = 2.78 mol m⁻² h⁻¹; ÷ 3600 s h⁻¹ = 7.7 × 10⁻⁴ mol m⁻² s⁻¹ = 0.77 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

3.5

State whether each change increases or decreases the transpiration rate when stomatal conductance stays the same.

  1. Lower air humidity
  2. Higher leaf temperature
  3. Stronger wind removing the boundary layer
  4. Smaller total leaf area
Answer
  1. Increases (larger vapour pressure difference between leaf and air).
  2. Increases (higher vapour pressure inside the leaf).
  3. Increases (thinner boundary layer, lower resistance).
  4. Decreases (less evaporating surface per plant).

Stomata

4.1

Put the steps of light-induced stomatal opening in order:

  1. guard-cell water potential falls
  2. blue light activates the guard-cell plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase
  3. turgor rises and the guard cells bow apart, opening the pore
  4. K⁺ enters through inward-rectifying channels, with Cl⁻ uptake and malate synthesis
  5. the membrane hyperpolarises
  6. water enters the guard cells
Answer

b → e → d → a → f → c.

4.2

State whether each condition causes stomata to open or close.

  1. Blue light at dawn
  2. High intercellular CO₂ concentration
  3. Increased ABA in the leaf
  4. Low intercellular CO₂ concentration
  5. Low leaf water potential (drought)
Answer
  1. Open. b. Close. c. Close. d. Open. e. Close.

4.3

How does abscisic acid (ABA) close stomata? Give the sequence from ABA perception to pore closure.

Answer

ABA binds receptors in the guard cells → anion channels open and anions leave → the membrane depolarises → K⁺ leaves through outward-rectifying channels → guard-cell Ψs and Ψ rise → water leaves → turgor falls → the pore closes. ABA acts as a signal; it does not plug the pore.

Mineral nutrition

5.1

Define an essential element.

Answer

An element without which the plant cannot complete its life cycle, or which is part of an essential molecule or structure of the plant; its function cannot be replaced by another element.

5.2

Classify each element as a macronutrient or a micronutrient: N, Fe, K, Zn, Mg, Mo, P, Mn, S, Cu, Ca, B.

Answer

Macronutrients: N, K, Mg, P, S, Ca. Micronutrients: Fe, Zn, Mo, Mn, Cu, B. The classification refers to the amount required, not to importance.

5.3

Match each element to one role.

Element
A Nitrogen
B Phosphorus
C Potassium
D Magnesium
E Iron

Roles: (1) central atom of chlorophyll; (2) main cation for osmotic regulation, e.g. in guard cells; (3) component of amino acids, proteins and nucleotides; (4) component of cytochromes and Fe–S proteins in electron transport; (5) component of ATP, nucleic acids and phospholipids.

Answer

A–3, B–5, C–2, D–1, E–4.

5.4

Deficiency of nitrogen first causes yellowing of the older leaves, whereas iron deficiency first causes yellowing of the young leaves. Explain the difference.

Answer

Nitrogen is mobile in the phloem: it is remobilised from old leaves to new growth, so old leaves show symptoms first. Iron is relatively immobile and cannot be withdrawn from old leaves, so new leaves, which depend on current uptake, show symptoms first.

5.5

What does each partner gain in an arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis?

Answer

The plant gains mineral nutrients, especially phosphate, and water taken up by the fungal hyphae from a larger soil volume. The fungus gains carbon (sugars and lipids) from the plant.

Explore: A plant cell in a solution

A flaccid plant cell is placed in a solution and exchanges water until its water potential Ψ = Ψs + Ψp equals that of the solution. Choose a preset or set the cell sap Ψs (at the flaccid volume), the solution Ψ and the wall stiffness; the bars and the readout give Ψs, Ψp and Ψ at equilibrium, and the label under the cell gives its state and protoplast volume.

E1.1

Click “Pure water” (cell sap Ψs −0.60 MPa, solution 0 MPa, wall stiffness 10 MPa). In which direction does water move, and what are Ψs, Ψp and Ψ of the cell at equilibrium?

Answer

Water moves into the cell. At equilibrium Ψ = 0 MPa, with Ψp = +0.57 MPa and Ψs = −0.57 MPa (the sap is slightly diluted as the protoplast swells to 1.06 × V₀). The cell is turgid.

E1.2

Click “−0.6 MPa” and then “−0.8 MPa”. Give the state of the cell (turgid, flaccid or plasmolysed) and its Ψp in each case.

Answer

−0.6 MPa: flaccid, Ψp = 0, no net water flow (the solution equals the cell sap). −0.8 MPa: plasmolysed, Ψp = 0; water leaves until Ψs has fallen to −0.80 MPa, at a protoplast volume of 0.75 × V₀.

E1.3

Click “Saline soil”: a root cell with Ψs = −0.40 MPa in a soil solution of −0.60 MPa. Does the cell gain or lose water, and what is its protoplast volume at equilibrium?

Answer

It loses water (−0.40 > −0.60) and plasmolyses; the protoplast shrinks to 0.67 × V₀ so that Ψs reaches −0.60 MPa. Even in wet soil, salt can lower the soil Ψ enough to draw water out of the root.

E1.4

Return to pure water (Ψs −0.60, solution 0) and compare wall stiffness 40 MPa with 2 MPa. Which wall gives the higher Ψp at equilibrium, and which cell swells more?

Answer

The stiff wall (40 MPa) gives the higher Ψp, +0.59 MPa, with almost no swelling (1.01 × V₀). The soft wall (2 MPa) swells to 1.24 × V₀, which dilutes the sap to −0.48 MPa, so Ψp is only +0.48 MPa.

E1.5

Keep the cell sap at −0.60 MPa and the wall at 10 MPa, and lower the solution Ψ slowly from 0. Below which solution Ψ does plasmolysis begin?

Answer

Below −0.60 MPa, i.e. when the solution Ψ is lower than the Ψs of the cell sap at the flaccid volume (incipient plasmolysis).

Explore: The soil–plant–air continuum

Steady-state transpiration through the chain soil → root → stem → leaf → air. The vapour pressure deficit and stomatal opening set the flux; each resistance along the path then lowers Ψ in proportion to it. Set humidity, soil Ψ, plant height and stomatal opening, or click a preset; the plot shows Ψ at each point, and the panel gives the transpiration rate.

E2.1

Click “Lecture” (50 % humidity, soil −0.10 MPa, 1 m, stomata fully open). Give the transpiration rate, Ψ of the leaf cells and Ψ of the air. Where along the path is the largest drop in water potential?

Answer

Transpiration 6.3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹, leaf cells −0.83 MPa, air −95 MPa. The largest drop is between the leaf and the air (about 94 MPa), in the vapour phase, far larger than any drop in the liquid path.

E2.2

Click “Humid” (90 % humidity). Does transpiration increase or decrease, and is the leaf Ψ higher or lower than at 50 %? What happens at 100 % humidity?

Answer

Transpiration decreases to 1.3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (the vapour pressure deficit falls from 1.58 to 0.32 kPa), and the leaf Ψ is higher, −0.25 MPa. At 100 % humidity the deficit is zero, transpiration stops and the profile is flat at the soil value.

E2.3

Click “Dry soil” (soil −0.80 MPa, stomata do not respond), then “Dry soil + ABA”. Give the transpiration rate and the leaf Ψ in each case.

Answer

Dry soil: 6.3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and leaf Ψ −5.47 MPa, far below the wilting line; the xylem would cavitate. Dry soil + ABA: stomata partly close (conductance 89 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹), transpiration falls to 1.4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and the leaf stays at −1.85 MPa. The plant saves water at the cost of CO₂ uptake.

E2.4

Use the “Lecture” settings and halve stomatal opening to 50 %. By what factor does transpiration change, and does the leaf Ψ rise or fall?

Answer

Transpiration halves, from 6.3 to 3.1 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹; the leaf Ψ rises from −0.83 to −0.47 MPa because less water flows through the resistances.

E2.5

Click “Redwood” (100 m tall). How much water potential does gravity alone cost over that height, according to the panel?

Answer

0.98 MPa (0.0098 MPa per metre × 100 m), before any loss due to flow.
