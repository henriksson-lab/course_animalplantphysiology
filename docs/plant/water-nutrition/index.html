P2 — Water, minerals and transport

The question

How does water reach a leaf several metres above soil, and why can salt make water harder to obtain from wet soil? Explain water potential, root uptake, xylem transport and stomatal regulation. Connect mineral nutrition to root and shoot function, using units and compartments carefully.

Water moves down a potential gradient

Water potential, Ψ, expresses water’s chemical potential relative to a reference in pressure units. Here we use megapascals, MPa. Net passive movement occurs from higher to lower potential when a pathway is available. Less-negative values are higher: −0.2 MPa exceeds −0.8 MPa. Total potential determines direction, not solute concentration alone when pressure differs.

For a small hydrated-cell system we often simplify to:

\[\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p.\]

Solute potential Ψs becomes more negative as dissolved-particle concentration increases. Ψp is pressure potential. A fuller account includes gravity and matric effects, important for tall plants, soil and drying tissues. Omitting them is a stated approximation for this small system.

A cell with Ψs = −0.9 MPa and Ψp = +0.4 MPa has Ψ = −0.5 MPa. Surroundings at −0.2 MPa initially favour inward movement. As water enters, wall resistance can raise pressure and dilution can change solute potential. Initial values do not remain fixed. At equilibrium there is no net movement, although molecular exchange continues. Positive turgor can coexist with negative total Ψ.

Roots contain pathways and selective barriers

Water crosses tissues by apoplastic, symplastic and transmembrane routes. Aquaporins increase membrane permeability in appropriate states; they do not pump water uphill. Permeability affects how readily movement occurs, while the potential difference explains passive direction.

The endodermis surrounds the vascular region. Its Casparian strip restricts an uninterrupted wall-space route into the stele, helping ensure substances encounter selective membranes. Developing roots acquire additional barriers. Consequently, an entire root is not a uniform filter with constant permeability.

Roots take up mineral ions through channels and carriers. Plasma-membrane proton pumps use ATP to establish electrochemical gradients. Coupling to those gradients can drive uptake of other solutes against their own gradients. This is distinct from actively pumping water. Root respiration supports many uptake processes, so waterlogging can impair mineral nutrition when oxygen is limited even though water is abundant.

Evaporation connects leaves to xylem tension

Water evaporates from wet internal wall surfaces and vapour diffuses towards the atmosphere. Negative pressure can develop in the xylem water. Cohesion and adhesion help transmit tension through the conducting pathway. The cohesion–tension mechanism supports long-distance movement without a pump at each height.

Xylem water columns can become discontinuous through cavitation and embolism. Hydraulic efficiency and vulnerability depend on conduit structure and conditions. Root pressure contributes in some situations but is not a general explanation for supplying tall transpiring tree crowns.

Transpiration depends on energy, humidity, air movement, temperature and leaf area. A larger vapour pressure deficit can favour water loss if conductance is maintained. A chamber changes the leaf environment. Document these factors before attributing a difference entirely to plant genotype or treatment.

Stomata regulate carbon gain and water loss

A stoma is a pore bordered by guard cells. Ion transport changes guard-cell osmotic conditions, water movement and turgor; wall geometry converts these changes into pore movement. Light, CO2, water status and signals influence the response. Opening assists carbon acquisition while increasing vapour loss. Closing conserves water but can reduce carbon gain and evaporative cooling.

Abscisic acid, ABA, participates in drought signalling and stomatal closure. It influences ion-channel activity and other cellular processes rather than physically plugging a pore. Channel responses and subsequent growth changes operate on different timescales. Cuticle, rooting depth, hydraulic conductance and leaf area also matter. Similar stomatal conductance therefore does not guarantee identical whole-plant water status.

Essential minerals have specific roles

An essential element is required to complete the life cycle and has a role another element cannot simply replace. “Macro” and “micro” indicate typical required quantities rather than importance. Nitrogen contributes to amino acids and nucleotides; phosphorus to phosphorylated metabolites and nucleic acids; potassium to osmotic regulation and enzyme activity. Magnesium is part of chlorophyll, and iron participates in electron-transfer systems.

A yellow leaf does not uniquely diagnose one deficiency. Age, symptom pattern, element mobility, root condition and pH all matter. A readily remobilised element may leave old tissues to support new growth, making old leaves useful diagnostic clues. Nevertheless, symptoms require verification. Excess can also be harmful, and total soil content is not the same as root availability.

Mycorrhizal fungi can extend access to resources while receiving plant carbon. Nitrogen-fixing symbioses also connect resource acquisition to carbon supply. Benefits vary with host, partner and conditions. “Microbes improve growth” is an incomplete prediction unless the limiting resource and context are stated.

Drought and salinity have overlapping effects

Drying soil lowers water availability and can reduce hydraulic connectivity. Salts lower the solute (osmotic) potential and can cause ion toxicity or nutrient imbalance. Compatible-solute accumulation can contribute to osmotic adjustment but carries costs. Conserving water through closure can reduce carbon gain. A plant may survive a treatment without maintaining growth or reproduction.

Consider soil at −0.1 MPa versus saline soil solution at −0.6 MPa. A root cell initially at −0.4 MPa has a favourable inward gradient in the first setting and an outward one in the second, all else equal. Real compartments respond. Match measurement and claim

A pot loses water through soil evaporation as well as transpiration. A soil cover or appropriate unplanted control can help distinguish them. Five leaves from one pot do not supply five independent pots when watering was assigned to pots. Describe measurements at their actual level before choosing an analysis. Advanced statistics cannot manufacture independent treatment units.
