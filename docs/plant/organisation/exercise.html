P1 exercises — Building a functioning plant

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Cell compartments

1.1

Match each structure to its main function.

Structure
A Plasma membrane
B Primary cell wall
C Tonoplast
D Chloroplast
E Mitochondrion

Functions: (1) membrane surrounding the central vacuole; (2) site of photosynthesis; (3) selective control of exchange between the cytoplasm and the outside of the cell; (4) site of aerobic respiration; (5) resists deformation and constrains the direction of expansion.

Answer

A–3, B–5, C–1, D–2, E–4.

1.2

Define turgor pressure and state what happens to a non-woody shoot when turgor falls.

Answer

Turgor pressure is the positive hydrostatic pressure of the cell contents against the cell wall, produced when water enters a cell with a resisting wall. When turgor falls, the cells lose rigidity and the shoot wilts, even though the cells may still be alive.

1.3

Name three functions of the large central vacuole of a mature plant cell.

Answer

Any three of: storage of solutes (ions, sugars, organic acids, pigments, defensive compounds); generation of turgor by solute accumulation and water uptake; regulation of cytoplasmic pH; degradation of cellular material; allowing the cell to enlarge without making a corresponding volume of cytoplasm.

1.4

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. All plastids are chloroplasts.
  2. Leaf cells respire only in darkness.
  3. The cell wall is selectively permeable and controls which solutes enter the cell.
  4. Many root cells lack photosynthetically active chloroplasts.
Answer
  1. False. Chloroplasts are one type of plastid; others include amyloplasts (starch storage) and chromoplasts (pigments).
  2. False. Mitochondrial respiration continues in light and darkness.
  3. False. Water and small solutes pass through the wall; selective control is exercised by the plasma membrane.
  4. True.

Cell walls, growth and cell connections

2.1

Name the main load-bearing component of the primary cell wall and the polymer that reinforces secondary walls of xylem conduits.

Answer

Cellulose microfibrils (embedded in a matrix of hemicelluloses, pectins and proteins). Secondary walls of xylem conduits are reinforced by lignin.

2.2

Turgor pressure is present, but a cell does not enlarge irreversibly. Name the wall property that must change to allow growth, and the two materials that must be supplied as the cell enlarges.

Answer

The wall must be loosened so that it yields (extends irreversibly) under turgor. New wall material and new plasma membrane must be supplied.

2.3

Define apoplast, symplast and plasmodesma.

Answer
  • Apoplast: the continuous system of cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membranes (including the lumens of dead xylem conduits).
  • Symplast: the cytoplasm of cells connected through plasmodesmata.
  • Plasmodesma: a membrane-lined channel through the cell wall that connects the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.

2.4

A solute moves from the apoplast into the symplast. Which structure must it cross?

Answer

The plasma membrane of a cell.

3.3

Name the cell or tissue that matches each description.

3.5

Give the order in which CO₂ passes these leaf structures on its way from the atmosphere to the site of fixation: mesophyll cell, stomatal pore, chloroplast stroma, intercellular air space, plasma membrane.

Answer

Stomatal pore → intercellular air space → plasma membrane → mesophyll cell (cytosol) → chloroplast stroma. CO₂ dissolves in the water of the wet cell wall before crossing the plasma membrane.

3.6

Why does opening the stomata increase both CO₂ uptake and water loss?

Answer

CO₂ diffuses in and water vapour diffuses out through the same pore. The cuticle is nearly impermeable to both, so opening the pore raises the conductance for both gases at once.

Meristems and growth

4.1

Name the meristem responsible for each of the following.

  1. Increase in length of a root
  2. Production of secondary xylem and secondary phloem
  3. Production of the protective outer bark tissue (periderm)
  4. Regrowth of a grass leaf after its tip has been cut
Answer
  1. Root apical meristem. b. Vascular cambium (secondary xylem inward, secondary phloem outward). c. Cork cambium (phellogen). d. Intercalary (basal) meristem.

4.2

A root segment contains 100 cells arranged end to end. Ignore spaces between cells.

  1. Calculate the segment length in mm when the mean cell length is 40 µm.
  2. With no cell division, the mean cell length becomes 70 µm. Calculate the new segment length and the increase.
  3. How many 40 µm cells would give the same final length as in (b)?
Answer
  1. 100 × 40 µm = 4000 µm = 4 mm.
  2. 100 × 70 µm = 7000 µm = 7 mm; increase = 3 mm.
  3. 7000 µm / 40 µm = 175 cells. The same length can result from cell expansion or from cell division, so length alone does not identify the process.

4.3

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Cell differentiation requires a change in the DNA sequence.
  2. Primary growth increases the length of roots and shoots.
  3. Every mature plant cell can regenerate a whole plant under any conditions.
Answer
  1. False. Differentiation results from differences in gene expression; the DNA sequence is unchanged.
  2. True.
  3. False. Some cells can resume division and regenerate, but this depends on genotype, developmental state and culture conditions.

Three genomes

5.1

Name the three genomes of a plant cell and state which one encodes most of the proteins that function inside chloroplasts.

Answer

Nuclear, plastid (chloroplast) and mitochondrial genomes. Most chloroplast proteins are encoded in the nucleus, synthesised in the cytosol and imported into the chloroplast.

5.2

A mutation in a nuclear gene encoding a chloroplast-imported protein reduces photosynthesis, while the chloroplast DNA is unchanged. Is this possible? Explain in one sentence.

Answer

Yes. The protein functions in the chloroplast but its gene is in the nucleus, so a nuclear mutation can impair chloroplast function.

5.3

Leaf and root cells of one plant have the same nuclear genome but different functions. What is the reason?

Answer

They express different sets of genes (tissue-specific gene expression).

Explore: Growth of a walled cell

The demo models one elongating cell with the Lockhart equation, relative growth rate r = φ(P − Y), where P is turgor, Y the wall yield threshold (both in MPa) and φ the wall extensibility (MPa⁻¹ h⁻¹). Set the sliders or click a preset; the readout gives r and the doubling time, and “Grow for 8 h” grows the cell from 20 µm.

E1.1

Use the default settings (P = 0.60, Y = 0.30, φ = 1.0). What is the growth rate r, the doubling time, and the cell length after 8 h (to the nearest µm)?

Answer

r = 1.0 × (0.60 − 0.30) = 0.30 h⁻¹; doubling time ln 2 / 0.30 = 2.3 h; length after 8 h = 20 × e^(0.30 × 8) ≈ 220 µm.

E1.2

Click “Stiff wall: P < Y” (P = 0.60, Y = 0.70). The cell is fully turgid. What is the growth rate, and why?

Answer

r = 0. Turgor (0.60 MPa) does not exceed the yield threshold (0.70 MPa), so the wall only stretches elastically and does not yield. A turgid cell is not necessarily a growing cell.

E1.3

Keep Y = 0.30. Which change gives the larger growth rate: (a) doubling φ to 2.0 with P = 0.60, or (b) raising P to 0.90 with φ = 1.0?

Answer

Neither: both give r = 0.60 h⁻¹ (2.0 × 0.30 and 1.0 × 0.60), with a doubling time of 1.2 h. Doubling the extensibility has the same effect as doubling the excess of turgor over the yield threshold.

E1.4

Click “Acid growth (auxin)” (P = 0.60, Y = 0.20, φ = 1.5). What is r, and by what factor is it higher than in the default? Which wall proteins, activated by low wall pH, does this preset represent?

Answer

r = 1.5 × (0.60 − 0.20) = 0.60 h⁻¹, twice the default 0.30 h⁻¹. Turgor is unchanged; the wall was loosened. Auxin stimulates plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPases, the wall acidifies and expansins loosen the cellulose–hemicellulose network (higher φ, lower Y).

E1.5

Click “Low turgor” (P = 0.35, Y = 0.30, φ = 1.0), as in a mildly water-stressed root. What is r and the doubling time? Roughly how long is the cell after 8 h?

Answer

r = 1.0 × 0.05 = 0.05 h⁻¹, doubling time 13.9 h; after 8 h the cell is only about 30 µm (20 × e^0.4 ≈ 29.8 µm). A small drop in turgor nearly stops growth because only the excess above Y drives expansion.

Explore: Root growth, more cells or longer cells?

Two root tips, A and B, are shown with the tip held still on the right. Each meristem produces new cells at a set rate per cell file; each cell leaves the meristem at 10 µm, elongates to a set final length and then matures. Set the two sliders for each root and press “Grow for 8 h”; the readouts give the number of new cells, the distance the tip moved, and the growth rate.

E2.1

Use the “Slide example” (A: 2.2 cells/h, final length 40 µm; B: 1.25 cells/h, 70 µm) and grow for 8 h. How many new cells does each root make, and how far does each tip move?

Answer

A: 17 new cells, B: 10 new cells. Both tips move 0.70 mm (growth rates 2.2 × 40 = 88 µm/h and 1.25 × 70 = 87.5 µm/h). Root length alone cannot tell the two roots apart.

E2.2

A drug that blocks cell division halves root A’s cell production to 1.1 cells/h without changing the final cell length (40 µm). What is A’s growth rate, and how far does its tip move in 8 h?

Answer

1.1 × 40 = 44 µm/h (0.044 mm/h); the tip moves 0.35 mm in 8 h, half as far as before.

E2.3

Set root B’s cell production to 1.10 cells/h. Which final cell length (slider in steps of 5 µm) makes B grow at exactly the same rate as the default root A?

Answer

80 µm: 1.1 × 80 = 88 µm/h = 2.2 × 40. Both tips then move 0.70 mm in 8 h.

E2.4

Click “Identical roots” (1.5 cells/h, 60 µm). Then set A’s final cell length to 120 µm, as in a mutant with more extensible walls. By what factor does A’s growth rate change, and which zone of the root tip is responsible?

Answer

It doubles (from 1.5 × 60 = 90 µm/h to 1.5 × 120 = 180 µm/h). The change is in the elongation zone, where cells expand; the meristem still produces the same number of cells.

R3. Surface area and volume of a growing cell

Treat a root cell as a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(L\): surface area \(= 2\pi r L + 2\pi r^2\) and volume \(= \pi r^2 L\). Compare a young meristem cell (\(L\) = 10 µm) with an elongated cell (\(L\) = 150 µm), both with \(r\) = 5 µm (invented teaching dimensions). Run the code and answer:

  1. What is the surface-to-volume ratio (µm⁻¹) of each cell?
  2. How many times larger is the volume of the elongated cell?
  3. Which cell has less surface membrane per unit volume to serve?
r <- c(5, 5)          # radius, µm
L <- c(10, 150)       # length, µm: meristem cell, elongated cell
area   <- 2 * pi * r * L + 2 * pi * r^2
volume <- pi * r^2 * L
round(area / volume, 3)
round(volume[2] / volume[1], 1)
Answer 
[1] 0.600 0.413
[1] 15
  1. 0.600 µm⁻¹ for the meristem cell, 0.413 µm⁻¹ for the elongated cell.
  2. 15 times larger.
  3. The elongated cell. Much of its extra volume is the central vacuole, so the cell gets large without making 15 times more cytoplasm.

R5. Composition of the transit peptide

Chloroplast transit peptides are typically rich in serine (S) and threonine (T). Using transit and mature from R4, run the code. What fraction of the residues is S or T in (a) the transit peptide and (b) the mature protein?

frac_ST <- function(s) {
  aa <- strsplit(s, "")[[1]]
  mean(aa %in% c("S", "T"))
}
round(frac_ST(transit), 2)
round(frac_ST(mature), 2)
Answer
  1. 0.26; (b) 0.1 (R drops the trailing zero of 0.10).
[1] 0.26
[1] 0.1

The transit peptide has more than twice the S + T fraction of the mature protein, as expected for a chloroplast-targeting sequence.

R6. Translating and GC content

Below is a made-up DNA sequence and a small codon table containing only the codons it needs. Run the code and report (a) the protein sequence (one-letter code) and (b) the GC content in percent. Compare (a) with the first amino acids of rbcs in R4.

dna <- "ATGGCTTCCTCTATGCTC"   # a made-up DNA sequence
code <- c(ATG = "M", GCT = "A", TCC = "S", TCT = "S", CTC = "L")
starts <- seq(1, nchar(dna), by = 3)
codons <- substring(dna, starts, starts + 2)
codons
paste(code[codons], collapse = "")
gc <- strsplit(dna, "")[[1]]
round(100 * mean(gc %in% c("G", "C")), 1)
Answer
  1. MASSML; (b) 50 %.
[1] "ATG" "GCT" "TCC" "TCT" "ATG" "CTC"
[1] "MASSML"
[1] 50

MASSML is the start of RBCS-1A. Note that S is coded by two different codons (TCC and TCT): the genetic code is redundant, so one protein sequence can be written by many DNA sequences.
