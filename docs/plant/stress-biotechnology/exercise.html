P5 exercises — Stress, defence and biotechnology

The questions follow the tutorial and slides. Answer each question before opening its answer.

Abiotic stress

1.1

For each stress, name the main physiological problem it causes.

Stress Main problem
Drought
Salinity
Heat
Freezing
Answer
Stress Main problem
Drought Low soil water potential; reduced water uptake and loss of turgor
Salinity Osmotic effect (low soil water potential) plus ion toxicity, mainly from Na⁺ and Cl⁻
Heat Protein denaturation and increased membrane fluidity
Freezing Ice formation in extracellular spaces dehydrates cells; ice crystals can damage membranes

1.2

Which hormone accumulates in leaves during drought, and what is its main immediate effect?

Answer

Abscisic acid (ABA). It causes stomatal closure, which reduces transpiration.

1.3

What is the cost to the plant of the response in 1.2?

Answer

Closed stomata also reduce CO₂ uptake, so photosynthesis falls. Less transpiration also means less evaporative cooling, so leaf temperature rises.

1.4

Name two kinds of compatible solutes, and explain how accumulating them helps a plant under drought or salinity.

Answer

Examples are proline, glycine betaine and sugars or sugar alcohols such as sucrose and mannitol. They lower the cell’s solute potential, so the cell can keep taking up water from a soil with low water potential. They do so without inhibiting enzymes.

1.5

Name two classes of proteins induced by heat or drought stress that help protect cells.

Answer

Heat shock proteins (chaperones that refold or stabilise proteins) and dehydrins/LEA (late embryogenesis abundant) proteins. Antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase are also accepted.

1.6

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage cells. Name one other role they play in plants.

Answer

Signalling. For example, ROS act in stress signalling, stomatal closure and defence against pathogens.

1.7

Classify each as acclimation or evolutionary adaptation.

  1. Plants of a species become more freezing-tolerant after two weeks at 4 °C.
  2. A population on saline coastal soil has heritably higher salt tolerance than inland populations grown under the same conditions.
Answer
  1. Acclimation (cold acclimation). b. Evolutionary adaptation.

Biotic stress and defence

2.1

Name two constitutive (pre-existing) defences and two induced defences of plants.

Answer

Constitutive: the cuticle, cell walls, thorns or trichomes, and stored specialised metabolites. Induced: callose deposition, phytoalexin production, proteinase inhibitors, and the hypersensitive response.

2.2

Match each attacker to the main defence hormone pathway: salicylic acid (SA) or jasmonic acid (JA).

  1. A biotrophic pathogen that feeds on living tissue
  2. A chewing caterpillar
  3. A necrotrophic fungus that kills tissue before feeding on it
Answer
  1. SA. b. JA. c. JA, together with ethylene. The SA and JA pathways often act antagonistically.

2.3

What is the hypersensitive response, and why is it effective against biotrophic pathogens?

Answer

It is rapid, localised death of the plant cells at the infection site. A biotrophic pathogen needs living host cells, so killing the infected cells deprives it of nutrients and limits its spread.

2.4

What is systemic acquired resistance (SAR), and which hormone is required for it?

Answer

SAR is long-lasting, broad-spectrum resistance in uninfected parts of the plant after a local infection. It requires salicylic acid.

2.5

Name the three major classes of plant specialised (secondary) metabolites, and give one example of each.

Answer
  • Terpenoids, e.g. pyrethrins, essential oils such as menthol, or carotenoids.
  • Phenolics, e.g. flavonoids, tannins or lignin.
  • Nitrogen-containing compounds, e.g. alkaloids such as nicotine, caffeine or morphine, and cyanogenic glycosides.

2.6

Why are many defence compounds stored as inactive precursors, such as glucosinolates or cyanogenic glycosides?

Answer

The active compound is toxic to the plant itself. The precursor is stored separately from the activating enzyme, and they meet only when tissue is damaged by a herbivore. The toxin is therefore released only at the site of attack.

2.7

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. Defence compounds use carbon and nitrogen that could otherwise be used for growth.
  2. A plant with constitutively activated jasmonate signalling grows larger than a wild-type plant.
  3. Natural plant compounds are harmless to humans.
Answer
  1. True. This is the basis of the growth–defence trade-off.
  2. False. Constitutive defence activation usually reduces growth.
  3. False. Many, such as nicotine, ricin or cyanogenic glycosides, are toxic.

Model systems

3.1

Give three properties that make Arabidopsis thaliana a useful model plant.

Answer

Any three of: a short life cycle (about 6–8 weeks); small size; a small, fully sequenced genome; self-pollination; many seeds; easy transformation with Agrobacterium (floral dip); large collections of mutants.

Transformation and tissue culture

4.1

Put the steps of Agrobacterium-mediated transformation in order.

  1. Regenerate whole plants from the transformed cells
  2. Insert the gene of interest into the T-DNA of a binary vector
  3. Select transformed cells on medium containing an antibiotic or herbicide
  4. Co-cultivate plant explants with Agrobacterium
  5. Confirm the insertion and expression of the transgene
Answer

b → d → c → a → e.

4.2

What is a selectable marker gene? Give an example.

Answer

It is a gene, transferred together with the gene of interest, that allows only transformed cells to survive on a selective medium. Examples are nptII (kanamycin resistance), hpt (hygromycin resistance) and bar (resistance to the herbicide glufosinate).

4.3

What is the difference between stable transformation and transient expression?

Answer

In stable transformation, the transgene is integrated into the plant genome and inherited by the offspring. In transient expression, the introduced DNA is expressed for a few days without integration and is not inherited.

4.4

Name a method for delivering DNA into plant cells that does not use Agrobacterium.

Answer

Particle bombardment (biolistics). Protoplast transformation, using polyethylene glycol or electroporation, is also accepted.

4.5

What property of plant cells makes it possible to regenerate a whole plant from a single transformed cell?

Answer

Totipotency: many differentiated plant cells can dedifferentiate and regenerate all the organs of a whole plant.

4.6

In tissue culture, what does an explant form at each auxin-to-cytokinin ratio?

  1. High auxin : cytokinin
  2. Intermediate (roughly balanced)
  3. Low auxin : cytokinin (high cytokinin)
Answer
  1. Roots. b. Undifferentiated callus. c. Shoots. The exact concentrations depend on species and tissue.

4.7

Why must several independent transgenic lines be analysed, rather than one?

Answer

The T-DNA inserts at a random position in the genome. It can disrupt or activate a native gene, and tissue culture can itself cause mutations (somaclonal variation). A phenotype seen consistently in independent lines is more likely to be caused by the transgene.

Promoters and reporters

5.1

Match each promoter type to its expression pattern.

Promoter Pattern
a. Constitutive (e.g. CaMV 35S) 1. Expressed only after exposure to a signal
b. Tissue-specific 2. Expressed in nearly all tissues at all times
c. Inducible 3. Expressed only in particular cell types, e.g. guard cells
Answer

a–2, b–3, c–1.

5.2

Name two reporter genes used in plants, and how each one is detected.

Answer
  • GUS (β-glucuronidase): a blue product after staining with X-Gluc.
  • GFP (green fluorescent protein): fluorescence under blue/UV light.
  • Luciferase: light emission when luciferin is supplied.

Genetically modified crops

6.1

Match each GM trait to the gene or mechanism used.

Trait Gene or mechanism
a. Insect resistance 1. Bacterial EPSP synthase insensitive to glyphosate
b. Herbicide tolerance 2. Carotenoid biosynthesis genes in the endosperm
c. Golden Rice 3. Cry toxin gene from Bacillus thuringiensis
Answer

a–3, b–1, c–2. Golden Rice makes β-carotene (provitamin A) in the grain.

6.2

How does the Bt (Cry) protein kill insect larvae, and why is it harmless to mammals?

Answer

In the alkaline gut of susceptible larvae, the protoxin is activated by proteases. It binds to specific receptors on the gut epithelium and forms pores, so the gut cells lyse. Mammals have an acidic stomach and lack these receptors.

6.3

What is a refuge strategy in Bt crop cultivation, and what problem does it delay?

Answer

Non-Bt plants are grown next to the Bt crop. Susceptible insects survive on the refuge and mate with any resistant insects, which keeps the resistance alleles rare. This delays the evolution of Bt resistance in the pest.

6.4

How does genome editing with CRISPR–Cas9 differ from classical transgenesis?

Answer

Classical transgenesis inserts foreign DNA into the genome. CRISPR–Cas9 makes a targeted cut in an existing gene, which can create a small mutation in that gene. The Cas9 construct can then be removed by crossing, so the final plant may contain no foreign DNA.

6.5

Classify each question as empirical (it can be answered by measurement) or value-based.

  1. Does pollen from a GM crop reach a wild relative 100 m away?
  2. How much uncertainty about environmental effects is acceptable?
  3. Does a Bt crop reduce insecticide use in a region?
  4. Who should decide whether a GM crop is grown?
Answer
  1. Empirical. b. Value-based. c. Empirical. d. Value-based.

Explore: Stomata, ABA and drought

The demo shows a stoma closing as ABA rises in the guard cells, and how conductance, water loss, CO₂ uptake, CO₂ gained per water lost and leaf temperature (leaf minus air) change. Use the drought buttons or the ABA slider; “Air dryness” sets the vapour pressure deficit (VPD) of the air. The curves show water loss and CO₂ uptake as % of the fully open value.

E1.1

Keep air dryness at 2 kPa. Fill in the table from the readout for “Well watered”, “Mild drought” and “Severe drought”.

Conductance (mol m⁻² s⁻¹) Water loss (mmol m⁻² s⁻¹) CO₂ uptake (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) Leaf − air (°C)
Well watered (ABA 0)
Mild drought (ABA 1)
Severe drought (ABA 4)
Answer
Conductance Water loss CO₂ uptake Leaf − air
Well watered (ABA 0) 0.50 9.9 28.0 −0.8
Mild drought (ABA 1) 0.25 4.9 22.6 +4.2
Severe drought (ABA 4) 0.03 0.6 5.7 +8.5

Less transpiration means less evaporative cooling, so the leaf warms: with severe drought it is about 8 °C above air temperature.

E1.2

Compare well watered with mild drought.

  1. By what percentage does water loss fall, and by what percentage does CO₂ uptake fall?
  2. Does CO₂ gained per water lost increase or decrease? Give both values.
  3. Why does CO₂ uptake fall less than water loss?
Answer
  1. Water loss falls by 50 % (9.9 → 4.9); CO₂ uptake falls by only about 20 % (28.0 → 22.6).
  2. It increases, from 2.8 to 4.6 µmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O.
  3. Water loss is proportional to stomatal conductance, but CO₂ uptake is limited by two steps in series, the stomata and carboxylation in the chloroplast. Partly closing the stomata therefore costs less carbon than water.

E1.3

Set ABA to 0 and move air dryness from 2 to 4 kPa. Which of these change: conductance, water loss, CO₂ uptake, leaf temperature? Give the new water loss.

Answer

Water loss doubles to 19.7 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and the leaf cools (to −10.7 °C relative to the air). Conductance (0.50) and CO₂ uptake (28.0) do not change: in this model dry air drives transpiration through the same open stomata. Real plants also close their stomata partly in dry air.

Explore: Auxin and cytokinin in tissue culture

An explant grows on a medium with auxin and cytokinin. Set the two concentrations with the sliders (log scale, mg/L), drag the dot on the map, or use the preset buttons; the dish shows the explant after four weeks and the readout gives the outcome and the auxin : cytokinin ratio. “Infected by wild Agrobacterium” adds the hormones that a crown-gall T-DNA makes inside the plant cells. The model is a rule of thumb, not fitted to data.

E2.1

Press each preset and record the outcome and the auxin : cytokinin ratio: Rooting, Callus, Shoots, No hormones.

Answer
Preset Auxin (mg/L) Cytokinin (mg/L) Ratio Outcome
Rooting 2.0 0.020 100 Roots
Callus 1.0 1.0 1.0 Callus
Shoots 0.032 2.0 0.02 Shoots
No hormones 0.010 0.010 1.0 Little growth

High auxin : cytokinin gives roots, low gives shoots, and a balance gives undifferentiated callus (Skoog and Miller). The No-hormones medium has the same ratio as Callus, but too little of either hormone for growth: the absolute level matters too.

E2.2

Set cytokinin to 0.10 mg/L and auxin to 0.10 mg/L, then raise auxin in steps. At what auxin concentration does the outcome first change from Callus, and to what? What is the ratio then?

Answer

At 0.56 mg/L auxin it changes to “Roots from callus” (ratio 5.6); from 0.71 mg/L it is “Roots”. Up to 0.50 mg/L (ratio 5.0) the explant forms only callus. Swapping the two hormones gives the mirror result: shoots from callus once cytokinin reaches 0.56 mg/L at 0.10 mg/L auxin.

E2.3

Press “No hormones” and tick “Infected by wild Agrobacterium”. What does the explant form? Then press “Rooting” with the box still ticked: what is the outcome and the ratio now?

Answer

With no hormones in the medium it forms a tumour (crown gall), with ratio 1.0. On the rooting medium it is still a tumour, and the ratio falls from 100 to 2.9. The wild T-DNA carries genes for auxin and cytokinin synthesis, so the infected cells make their own hormones and proliferate whatever the medium. Disarmed strains used for transformation lack these genes.

Explore: Refuges and Bt resistance

The demo follows the frequency of a resistance allele R in a pest over generations. Bt maize kills 99 % of susceptible (SS) larvae; a refuge of non-Bt maize lets all genotypes survive, and adults from both mate at random. Set the refuge size, the dominance of R (h = 0: recessive, h = 1: dominant) and the starting frequency of R. The readout gives the number of generations until R reaches 50 % and the fitness of each genotype.

E3.1

Keep the starting frequency at 0.001 and h = 0. How many generations does it take for R to reach 50 % with no refuge, a 5 % refuge, a 10 % refuge and a 20 % refuge?

Answer
Refuge Generations to 50 % R
0 % 15
5 % 70
10 % 129
20 % more than 150

Susceptible adults from the refuge mate with the rare resistant survivors, so most R alleles end up in RS offspring, which are killed on Bt maize when R is recessive.

E3.2

Press “20% refuge”, then “20%, dominant R”. Read off the generations to 50 % and the fitness of RS for each. Which is the more important condition for the refuge to work?

Answer
RS fitness Generations to 50 % R
20 % refuge, h = 0 0.208 more than 150
20 % refuge, h = 1 1.000 6

With dominant resistance, RS larvae survive on Bt maize as well as RR, and the refuge no longer slows resistance (6 generations, faster even than no refuge with a recessive allele). The refuge strategy therefore requires a high-dose Bt crop that kills heterozygotes, which makes R effectively recessive.

E3.3

Set a 20 % refuge, h = 0, and the starting frequency of R to 0.01 instead of 0.001. How many generations does R take to reach 50 %?

Answer

31 generations (compared with more than 150 at 0.001). The refuge works best when R is very rare, because almost all R alleles are then in heterozygotes. Refuges must be in place from the first Bt planting, not added after resistance has been detected.

R3. Resistance evolution on a Bt crop

The loop follows the frequency p of a resistance allele R in an insect population feeding on a Bt crop. Fitness values are invented teaching numbers: RR larvae survive (w_RR = 1) and 10 % of SS larvae survive (w_SS = 0.1).

  1. Run the code with w_RS <- 1 (R dominant). What is the frequency of R after one generation (to four decimals), and after how many generations does it first exceed 0.5?
  2. Change to w_RS <- 0.1 (R recessive, as with a high-dose Bt crop). What does the last line print, and what does that mean?
  3. Keep w_RS <- 0.1 and change both 30s to 200. After how many generations does R first exceed 0.5?
p <- 0.001
w_RR <- 1; w_RS <- 1; w_SS <- 0.1
p_values <- p
for (gen in 1:30) {
  q <- 1 - p
  w_bar <- p^2 * w_RR + 2 * p * q * w_RS + q^2 * w_SS
  p <- (p^2 * w_RR + p * q * w_RS) / w_bar
  p_values <- c(p_values, p)
}
round(p_values[2], 4)
which(p_values > 0.5)[1] - 1
plot(0:30, p_values, type = "l", xlab = "Generation", ylab = "Frequency of R")
Answer 
  1. [1] 0.0098
[1] 4
    R rises from 0.001 to 0.0098 in one generation and exceeds 0.5 after 4 generations.
  2. [1] 0.001
[1] NA
    NA means no generation in the first 30 had a frequency above 0.5; R is still about 0.001.
  3. [1] 0.001
[1] 118
    118 generations. When RS larvae die like SS larvae, almost all rare R alleles (which sit in heterozygotes) are removed, so resistance spreads far more slowly. p_values[1] is generation 0, which is why the code subtracts 1.

R4. Finding a CRISPR guide next to a PAM

Cas9 cuts a DNA target that is directly followed by an NGG PAM (any base, then GG). A guide RNA matches the 20 nucleotides just before the PAM. The target below is a made-up sequence. Run the code. At which positions do NGG sites start, what is the 20-nucleotide guide before the first PAM, and what is its GC content (%)?

target <- "ATGCTAGCTTGACCGTAGCATCGGATCCAGTTAACGG"
pam <- as.vector(gregexpr("(?=[ACGT]GG)", target, perl = TRUE)[[1]])
pam
guide <- substr(target, pam[1] - 20, pam[1] - 1)
guide
bases <- strsplit(guide, "")[[1]]
gc <- mean(bases == "G" | bases == "C") * 100
gc
Answer 
[1] 22 35
[1] "TGCTAGCTTGACCGTAGCAT"
[1] 50

NGG sites start at positions 22 (CGG) and 35 (CGG). The guide is TGCTAGCTTGACCGTAGCAT (positions 2–21), with 50 % GC (10 of 20 bases are G or C). The pattern (?=...) lets R also report overlapping matches.

R5. Checking the edit: percent identity

After editing, the target region of a plant is sequenced and aligned with the wild type. Both are made-up sequences; - marks a gap. Run the code. How many aligned positions are identical, what is the percent identity (to one decimal), and at which position is the difference? Is the edit (a) a substitution, (b) a 1-base deletion or (c) a 1-base insertion, and does it shift the reading frame?

wild_type <- "ATGGCTTCAGGATCCAGTTAAGCT"
edited    <- "ATGGCTTCAGGAT-CAGTTAAGCT"
a <- strsplit(wild_type, "")[[1]]
b <- strsplit(edited, "")[[1]]
length(a)
sum(a == b)
round(sum(a == b) / length(a) * 100, 1)
which(a != b)
Answer 
[1] 24
[1] 23
[1] 95.8
[1] 14

23 of 24 positions are identical, 95.8 % identity, and the difference is at position 14. (b) a 1-base deletion: the C in the wild type faces a gap in the edited sequence. One base is not a multiple of three, so the reading frame shifts after the deletion. High percent identity therefore does not mean the protein is nearly unchanged.
