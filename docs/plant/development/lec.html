P4 — Signals, growth and reproduction

Week 9 — How a plant decides what to build, and when

Animal and Plant Physiology

About this lecture

By the end of P3 our plant has water, minerals and sugar. But a plant cannot move. It must decide when to germinate, which way to grow, whether to branch, when to flower and when to let a leaf die, using only what it can sense where it stands. How does a plant turn light, gravity and season into these decisions? Today we follow one plant from seed to senescence and ask, at each stage, which signal is in charge.

  • Signals: the chain from cue to response, and the main plant hormones.
  • Seed: dormancy, germination and the ABA : gibberellin balance.
  • Light as information: photoreceptors, and phytochrome as a red/far-red switch.
  • Growing the right way: phototropism, gravitropism and apical dominance.
  • Flowering: day length, night breaks, florigen and vernalisation.
  • The end of the cycle: double fertilisation and senescence.

Frame the lecture as a life cycle: seed, seedling, shoot, flower, seed again, and finally an ageing leaf. At each stage the plant makes a decision, and the question is always the same: what does it sense, what carries the signal, and what does the signal change? Link back to P3: carbon and nutrients are the raw material; today is about the control of how that material is used. P5 then uses the same signalling machinery for stress and defence.

From cue to response

  • Every response needs a receptor. A cue the plant cannot perceive has no effect.
  • Fast responses (seconds to minutes) modify existing proteins: stomata closing, a pulvinus folding.
  • Slow responses (hours to weeks) change gene expression: a flower forming.
  • The same signal can give different responses in different tissues.

Many of the signals inside the plant are hormones. Which ones?

This is the framework for every example in the lecture: whenever we meet a response, ask what the cue is, what the receptor is, and whether the change is in existing proteins or in gene expression. Point out the last bullet: auxin promotes elongation in shoots but inhibits it in roots at the same concentration, so the response depends on the responding cell, not only on the signal. The next slide lists the hormones.

Students must be able to match hormone and effect (exercise 1.1). Go through the example column, because concrete cases are easier to recall than lists: rooting powder is synthetic auxin; ethylene from one ripe fruit triggers ripening in its neighbours in climacteric fruits such as banana, tomato and apple, but not in non-climacteric fruits such as citrus or grape. Strigolactones are included because they appear on the apical dominance slide. Transition: how do we find out what a hormone does? Mostly from mutants.

Reading a hormone mutant

Spray gibberellin on two dwarf pea mutants:

Mutant Response to GA Defect
A Grows to normal height Biosynthesis: cannot make GA (Mendel’s le dwarf)
B Stays dwarf Perception or signalling: cannot respond to GA

Ethylene and dark-grown seedlings: the triple response

  • Wild type + ethylene: short, thick hypocotyl and an exaggerated apical hook.
  • etr1 (ethylene receptor mutant) + ethylene: long, thin hypocotyl, normal hook. The hormone is present, but the receptor cannot pass on the signal.

Rule: if adding the hormone rescues the mutant, the defect is in making it.

This is the logic of hormone genetics and appears in exercises 1.4 and 1.5. A biosynthesis mutant is rescued by the hormone; a receptor or signalling mutant is not. The Green Revolution semi-dwarf wheats (Rht genes) are GA-insensitive, which is the second class. The triple response screen found the ethylene receptor ETR1: mutants that ignore ethylene look like untreated seedlings. Transition: we start the life cycle, with the seed, where two of these hormones are in direct opposition.

The seed: dormant or just waiting?

Quiescent Dormant
Definition Will germinate as soon as conditions allow Will not germinate even in water, O₂ and warmth
Example Dry seeds in a packet; seeds in dry soil Fresh seeds that need months of dry storage
Released by Water After-ripening (dry storage), stratification (moist cold), light
  • ABA maintains dormancy; gibberellin promotes germination. The balance decides.
  • ABA-deficient maize mutants show vivipary: seeds germinate on the cob.
  • GA-deficient mutants do not germinate unless GA is supplied.
  • Germination (radicle emergence) is scored at a stated time: 12 of 20 seeds by day 5 = 60%.

Dormancy is an internal block; quiescence is only a lack of water or warmth. Stratification is why many temperate seeds germinate in spring after a winter in moist soil, not in autumn. The two mutant phenotypes show the ABA : GA balance from both sides (exercise 4.2). The germination percentage needs a denominator and a time point (exercise 4.3); non-germinated seeds may be dormant, slow or dead. Transition: once germination starts, what does gibberellin actually do in the seed?

Germination: gibberellin mobilises the reserves

  • In a barley grain the starchy endosperm is dead tissue. The living aleurone layer around it makes the enzymes.
  • The embryo signals with GA; the aleurone responds by transcribing α-amylase.
  • ABA blocks this response, so a dormant grain keeps its reserves.
  • The malting industry uses exactly this: barley is germinated, with added GA, to produce sugar for brewing.

But many small seeds also check something outside: is there light?

This is the best-understood hormone response in a seed and is the order asked in exercise 1.3: GA from embryo, induction in the aleurone, secretion, hydrolysis, uptake via the scutellum. It is also a clear example of a slow, gene-expression response from the first slide. ABA antagonism ties back to the ABA : GA balance. Transition: small seeds such as lettuce have few reserves and should only germinate near the surface, which they detect with light.

Light is information as well as energy

Photoreceptor Light absorbed Example responses
Phytochrome Red / far-red Seed germination, shade avoidance, photoperiod
Cryptochrome Blue / UV-A Inhibits hypocotyl elongation; entrains the circadian clock
Phototropin Blue / UV-A Phototropism, chloroplast movement, stomatal opening
UVR8 UV-B UV-protective responses (e.g. flavonoid sunscreens)
  • A seedling responds to a light flash far too dim to support photosynthesis. These receptors read the colour, direction and timing of light.
  • A dark-grown seedling is etiolated: long, pale, hooked. Light triggers de-etiolation: the hook opens, leaves expand, chlorophyll forms.

The red/far-red receptor is the key to germination and to flowering. How does it work?

Distinguish light as energy (P3) from light as a signal. The match between photoreceptor, wavelength and response is exercise 3.4. Etiolation is a strategy: a buried seedling puts all its reserves into elongation until it reaches light. Phytochrome gets the next three slides because it underlies germination, shade avoidance and the night-break experiments in flowering.

Phytochrome: a red/far-red switch

Pr Pfr
Absorbs Red, ~660 nm Far-red, ~730 nm
Converted by light to Pfr Pr
Biologically active? No Yes
Synthesised as? Yes No
  • Red light → mostly Pfr (active). Far-red light → mostly Pr (inactive).
  • The conversion is photoreversible: the last light treatment decides the state.
  • Active Pfr moves into the nucleus and changes gene expression.
  • In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts or is degraded, so the Pfr level falls overnight.

Students should be able to fill this table from memory (exercise 3.1). The key idea is a switch with two stable states, each converted to the other by the light it absorbs. Phytochrome is made as inactive Pr, which is why a seed in darkness has no active phytochrome. The last bullet matters for photoperiod: the plant can use the decline in Pfr during the night. The next slide shows the classic experiment that proved photoreversibility.

The lettuce seed experiment

Imbibed lettuce seeds, brief light flashes, then darkness:

Flash sequence Last flash Phytochrome Germinate?
Dark only none Pr No
R R Pfr Yes
R → FR FR Pr No
R → FR → R R Pfr Yes
R → FR → R → FR FR Pr No
  • The last flash decides. This photoreversibility identified phytochrome (Borthwick and Hendricks, 1950s).
  • Why it matters: a buried seed sees no light and stays dormant until the soil is disturbed.

Light under a canopy has a different colour. What does phytochrome tell the plant there?

Work the sequence column by column; exercise 3.2 extends it to five flashes. The ecological meaning: small-seeded weeds germinate after ploughing because the soil is turned and seeds get a flash of light. Transition to shade: phytochrome does not just detect light versus dark, it detects the ratio of red to far-red.

Shade avoidance

  • Chlorophyll absorbs red light but transmits and reflects far-red.
  • Under a canopy, the red : far-red ratio falls (sunlight ~1.2; deep shade ~0.2).
  • Less red → less Pfr. The plant reads this as “neighbours above me”.
Shade-avoidance response Benefit
Faster stem and petiole elongation Leaves reach light before the competitor’s
Leaves angled upwards Less self-shading
Less branching Resources go into height
Earlier flowering Seeds are set before resources run out
  • The cost: thin, weak stems and less root growth. Dense crops lose yield to this.

Exercise 3.3. Emphasise that the plant responds to reflected far-red from neighbours even before it is actually shaded, which is an early warning. The cost row connects to agriculture: crop breeders have selected plants with weaker shade avoidance to allow dense planting. Transition: phytochrome reads colour; the direction of light is read by a different receptor and causes the shoot to bend.

Phototropism: bending towards light

Coleoptile treatment, lit from one side Bends?
Untreated Yes
Tip removed, or covered with an opaque cap No
Tip in a transparent cap Yes
Opaque collar below the tip, tip exposed Yes
Tip cut off and replaced on a gelatin block Yes
  • Light is perceived at the tip, by phototropin (blue light).
  • A diffusible signal, auxin, moves down to the growing zone.
  • Cholodny–Went model: auxin is moved to the shaded side; cells there elongate faster, so the shoot bends towards the light.

The table summarises Darwin (1880, caps and collars) and Boysen-Jensen (1913, gelatin block); Went (1928) then collected the signal in agar blocks and named it auxin. Each row asks: where is light perceived, and can the signal pass through gelatin? This is exercises 2.1 and 2.2. Transition: the same auxin redistribution explains a response to gravity, but in roots auxin has the opposite effect.

Gravitropism: the root grows down

Root turned horizontal:

  1. Columella cells of the root cap perceive gravity.
  2. Starch-filled amyloplasts (statoliths) sediment to the lower side.
  3. Auxin is redirected to the lower side of the root.
  4. In roots, high auxin inhibits elongation.
  5. The upper side grows faster: the root bends down.

Shoot turned horizontal:

  • Auxin also accumulates on the lower side.
  • In shoots, auxin promotes elongation.
  • The lower side grows faster: the shoot bends up.

Same signal, same redistribution, opposite outcome: the response depends on the tissue’s sensitivity to auxin.

Exercise 2.3. The root cap is the sensor: remove it and the root still grows but no longer bends. The contrast between root and shoot is the clearest demonstration of the first slide’s point that the response depends on the responding tissue. Transition: auxin flowing down the shoot also decides whether side branches grow.

Apical dominance: should the plant branch?

Pea shoot treatment Lateral buds
Intact Inhibited
Apex removed Grow out
Apex removed + auxin paste on stump Inhibited
Apex removed + plain lanolin on stump Grow out
Intact + cytokinin on a bud Grows out
  • Auxin from the apex replaces the apex (Thimann and Skoog, 1934).
  • Auxin does not enter the bud. It acts indirectly: it lowers cytokinin supply to the bud and raises strigolactone synthesis.
  • Gardeners use this: pinching out the tip makes a bushier plant.

The shoot is now built. When does it stop making leaves and make flowers?

Exercise 1.2. The lanolin-only control is essential: it shows that the paste itself does nothing, so the effect is due to auxin. The indirect mechanism is the modern view; the old idea that auxin enters the bud and inhibits it directly does not fit the transport data. Transition to flowering: the biggest developmental decision of an annual plant.

Flowering: measuring the night

  • Short-day plants (SDP) flower when the night is longer than a critical night length (chrysanthemum, soybean, poinsettia).
  • Long-day plants (LDP) flower when the night is shorter than it (Arabidopsis, spinach, wheat).
  • What the plant measures is the uninterrupted dark period.

Critical night length 11 h:

24 h cycle Longest dark period SDP LDP
16 h light / 8 h dark 8 h No Yes
8 h light / 16 h dark 16 h Yes No
8 h light (with 1 h dark in the middle) / 16 h dark 16 h Yes No

What happens if we interrupt the night instead of the day?

The names are historical: they refer to day length, but experiments showed that night length is what is measured. The third row shows that darkness during the day does nothing, because the night is unchanged. Exercise 5.1 uses exactly this setup. Transition: a brief light flash in the middle of the night has a large effect, and the colour of that flash tells us which receptor reads the night.

Night breaks reveal phytochrome

8 h light / 16 h dark, with a brief flash in the middle of the night:

Night break Phytochrome after flash Longest dark period (as perceived) SDP LDP
None — 16 h Yes No
Red Pfr 8 h No Yes
Red → far-red Pr 16 h Yes No
  • A few minutes of red light splits a long night into two short ones.
  • Far-red immediately after reverses the red effect, so phytochrome is the receptor.
  • Commercial use: growers light chrysanthemums at night to delay flowering until the market wants them.

Exercise 5.1 c and d. Connect to the lettuce seed slide: the same last-flash rule, applied to a night instead of a seed. Students often think the red flash adds “day”; stress that it only matters because it breaks the dark period. Transition: day length is measured in the leaves, but flowers form at the shoot apex. How does the message get there?

Florigen: a protein from leaf to apex

  • Grafting experiments in the 1930s showed that an induced leaf sends a signal, named florigen.
  • In Arabidopsis, florigen is the protein FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT). FT tagged with GFP is made in leaves and found at the apex (Corbesier et al., 2007).
  • The leaf perceives, the phloem transports, the apex responds.

Exercise 5.2. CONSTANS protein accumulates in the leaf phloem companion cells only when long days give light at the end of the day, which is how day length is converted into a molecular signal. FT is small enough to move through plasmodesmata into sieve tubes and travels with the assimilate flow from P3. At the apex it pairs with the transcription factor FD to switch on floral identity genes such as APETALA1. Transition: some plants need a second condition before they can respond to day length, namely a winter.

Vernalisation: remembering winter

  • Vernalisation: prolonged cold (weeks at ~0–10 °C) gives the competence to flower. Examples: winter wheat, winter-annual Arabidopsis.
  • The plant does not flower in the cold. It flowers later, in warm, long days.
  • The cold is perceived by the shoot apex.
  • In Arabidopsis, cold represses FLC, a repressor of flowering. FLC stays off through later cell divisions: an epigenetic memory of winter.
  • The memory is reset in each generation: seeds need cold again.
Plant Cold received Outcome in spring long days
Winter wheat sown in autumn Yes Flowers, sets grain
Winter wheat sown in spring No Stays vegetative

Exercise 5.3 is a true/false set on these bullets; the common misconceptions are that cold triggers flowering directly and that the vernalised state is inherited. The point of vernalisation is to prevent flowering in autumn, when days are short in the run-up to winter; flowering waits for the spring after a winter has passed. Transition: once the flower opens and is pollinated, the next generation begins.

Double fertilisation: building the seed

Structure Formed from Ploidy
Zygote → embryo Sperm cell 1 + egg cell 2n
Endosperm Sperm cell 2 + central cell (two polar nuclei) 3n
Seed coat Integuments of the ovule (maternal) 2n
Fruit wall (pericarp) Ovary wall (maternal) 2n
  • The pollen tube delivers two sperm cells; both fertilise: double fertilisation.
  • The endosperm is the food store we met in the barley grain.
  • The seed coat and fruit are maternal tissue, not the next generation.
  • Developing seeds are strong sinks (P3): they draw sugar and nutrients from the leaves.

Where do those nutrients come from at the end of the season?

Exercise 5.4. Close the loop to the start of the lecture: the endosperm made here is the starch store mobilised by gibberellin in the next generation. The ploidy column is the usual exam check. The sink bullet links back to phloem transport in P3 and leads into senescence: the old leaves become the nutrient source for the seeds.

Senescence: recycling the leaf

  • Senescence is a regulated, programmed process, not passive decay.

  • Chlorophyll and proteins (including Rubisco) are broken down first, so the leaf yellows.

  • Nutrients are remobilised through the phloem to seeds and young leaves.

  • N, P and K are phloem-mobile and are exported. Ca is phloem-immobile and stays in the leaf.

Hormone Effect on senescence
Cytokinin Delays it
Ethylene, ABA, jasmonic acid Promote it
  • Tobacco expressing a cytokinin-synthesis gene from a senescence promoter keeps its leaves green much longer (Gan and Amasino, 1995).

Exercise 6.1. The phloem-mobility rule comes from P2: calcium moves in the xylem only, so it cannot be exported from an old leaf, and calcium deficiency appears in young tissue. A yellow leaf alone does not show senescence; deficiency or stress also yields yellowing, so tissue age and growth conditions must be known. The Gan and Amasino experiment is a neat feedback loop: senescence switches on cytokinin production, which delays senescence. It also introduces the idea of a stage-specific promoter, which returns in P5.
