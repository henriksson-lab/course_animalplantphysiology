P3 — Light, carbon and the whole-plant budget

The question

A well-lit leaf exports sugar while a root consumes it. What connects the two, and why can the same leaf release CO2 in darkness? Explain energy capture, carbon fixation, transport, respiration and nutrient assimilation. Interpret a response without assuming that light is its only limitation.

Capturing energy differs from fixing carbon

Pigments in chloroplast thylakoids absorb light. Excitation reaches reaction centres, and electron transfer supports a proton electrochemical gradient. ATP synthase uses that gradient to form ATP. Water supplies electrons to photosystem II and oxygen is released; photosynthetic oxygen does not come directly from splitting CO2.

Linear electron flow supports ATP and NADPH formation. Cyclic flow can contribute additional ATP without equivalent net NADPH or oxygen production. ATP and reducing power are intermediate resources, not the final carbohydrate supply for the entire plant. Their production must be coordinated with use.

The Calvin–Benson cycle operates in the stroma. Rubisco adds CO2 to ribulose bisphosphate. Subsequent reactions use ATP and NADPH to reduce the products; some carbon becomes available for synthesis and export while the rest regenerates the acceptor. In standard cycle accounting, three fixed CO2 and nine ATP plus six NADPH support one net triose-phosphate equivalent. This does not represent every energetic cost of building a plant.

“Dark reactions” is a misleading name. These reactions do not consume photons directly, but light-dependent resources and regulation support them. Ordinary carbon fixation cannot continue indefinitely in darkness just because the cycle is described as light-independent.

Oxygenation imposes additional costs

Rubisco can react with oxygen instead of CO2. The resulting photorespiratory pathway recovers carbon while releasing some CO2 and consuming energy. It involves chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria. Photorespiration is distinct from mitochondrial respiration, even though both can contribute CO2 release. Under relevant conditions, warming and reduced internal CO2 favour a greater relative oxygenation rate.

Water stress can close stomata and reduce CO2 supply. Net uptake may decline despite bright light. Later limitations may involve metabolism or tissue damage. A lower measured rate alone cannot identify stomatal, photochemical or biochemical limitation. It is an integrated outcome requiring more evidence.

C3, C4 and CAM change carbon delivery

In C3 plants, initial Rubisco products are three-carbon compounds. C4 plants first capture inorganic carbon through PEP carboxylase into four-carbon compounds. In the common two-cell arrangement, these compounds release CO2 near Rubisco in bundle-sheath cells. Concentrating CO2 can suppress photorespiration but costs additional energy. Warm, bright conditions often favour this strategy; it is not universally superior.

CAM plants commonly take up CO2 at night and store carbon in organic acids, then release it for fixation during the day. Separating these steps in time can reduce water loss. Storage and rate constraints remain. C4 and CAM deliver concentrated CO2 to the Calvin–Benson cycle rather than replacing it.

Predict different evidence for each hypothesis. A nocturnal rise in acid content informs a CAM hypothesis; anatomy and gas-exchange responses inform a C4 hypothesis. A single observation may not establish the entire pathway. Some species show intermediate or flexible behaviour.

A response curve describes a balance

Net CO2 exchange balances uptake and release under the measurement conditions. At low light, increased irradiance can increase net uptake. The light compensation point is the condition at which uptake and release balance. At greater light, CO2 supply, biochemical capacity or another process can become limiting. Excess excitation can be dissipated protectively; damage exceeding repair can cause photoinhibition.

Imagine invented net rates of −2, 0, 6 and 9 µmol CO2 m⁻² s⁻¹ at 0, 50, 200 and 800 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Compensation is near the second observation. The smaller response per additional photon at high light does not prove which process limits it. Record temperature, CO2, humidity, leaf age and measurement order. Repeated conditions on one leaf are not separate independent plants.

Pigment concentration, chlorophyll fluorescence and CO2 exchange measure different properties. Fluorescence informs photochemical behaviour, whereas net gas exchange includes additional processes. A greener leaf need not have proportionally greater carbon uptake.

Sources and sinks change with development

A source exports assimilate; a sink imports it for use or storage. A mature illuminated leaf is commonly a source and a root or developing fruit a sink. A storage organ can become a source during regrowth. These are current carbon roles rather than immutable organ identities.

Phloem loading raises solute content near a source, favouring water entry and pressure development. Unloading and carbon use at sinks help maintain a pressure difference driving bulk flow. Species differ in loading routes. Different tubes can carry assimilate in different directions, so phloem is not simply an exclusively downward counterpart to xylem.

This original schematic separates carbon roles from organ location. It omits many transport steps and does not imply that every species loads identically.

Starch can bridge day and night. Storage, export and growth demand are regulated together; reduced sink demand can change source activity. Transport is not independent of flowering, seed filling or senescence.

Respiration supplies more than CO2

Glycolysis, the tricarboxylic-acid cycle and mitochondrial electron transport connect carbohydrate breakdown to ATP and metabolic intermediates. Plants respire in light and darkness. Roots use carbon for uptake, maintenance and growth; leaves also require mitochondrial function. Respiration is not merely wasteful reversal of photosynthesis.

When oxygen is restricted, oxidative ATP generation is limited. Fermentation can regenerate NAD+ and permit continued glycolysis, with much lower ATP yield than complete aerobic oxidation. Waterlogged roots can therefore develop energy and transport limitations despite well-lit leaves. Many seeds also use lipid reserves during establishment, connecting reserve mobilisation and organelle metabolism to developmental stage.

Nitrate must be reduced before incorporation into organic nitrogen. Ammonium assimilation requires carbon skeletons and energy. Nutrient supply can alter photosynthetic capacity, while carbon supply can limit nutrient assimilation. Whole-plant budgets require both sides. More nitrogen cannot necessarily compensate for inadequate light or lack of oxygen at roots.
