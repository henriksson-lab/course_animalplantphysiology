1.2

The O₂ released in photosynthesis comes from:

  1. CO₂
  2. H₂O
  3. glucose
  4. ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate
Answer
  1. Water is oxidised at photosystem II, supplying electrons and releasing O₂. CO₂ supplies the carbon of the sugars.

1.3

Put the carriers of linear (non-cyclic) electron flow in order, starting with water: ferredoxin, photosystem I, plastoquinone, NADP⁺, photosystem II, plastocyanin, cytochrome b₆f complex.

Answer

H₂O → photosystem II → plastoquinone → cytochrome b₆f complex → plastocyanin → photosystem I → ferredoxin → NADP⁺ (via ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase).

1.4

1.5

How is the energy of the proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane used, and in which direction do protons flow through the enzyme?

Answer

ATP synthase uses it to make ATP from ADP and Pᵢ. Protons flow from the thylakoid lumen (high H⁺) to the stroma (low H⁺), so ATP is formed in the stroma.

The Calvin–Benson cycle and photorespiration

2.1

Name the three phases of the Calvin–Benson cycle in order, and the enzyme that catalyses the first.

Answer

Carboxylation → reduction → regeneration of ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). Carboxylation is catalysed by Rubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase), which adds CO₂ to RuBP to form two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate.

2.2

Fixing 3 CO₂ into one net triose phosphate requires 9 ATP and 6 NADPH.

  1. How much ATP and NADPH are needed to fix 6 CO₂, the carbon of one hexose?
  2. What is the ATP : NADPH ratio required?
Answer
  1. 18 ATP and 12 NADPH.
  2. 3 : 2 (1.5 ATP per NADPH).

2.3

True or false? Correct any false statements.

  1. The Calvin–Benson cycle runs all night because it is a “dark reaction”.
  2. Rubisco can react with O₂ as well as CO₂.
  3. Photorespiration is the same process as mitochondrial respiration.
  4. Photorespiration releases CO₂ that has already been fixed.
Answer
  1. False. The cycle needs ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, and several of its enzymes are activated in light; it stops soon after darkness begins.
  2. True.
  3. False. Photorespiration starts with Rubisco oxygenation and recycles 2-phosphoglycolate; mitochondrial respiration oxidises sugars to make ATP.
  4. True. CO₂ is released in the mitochondria when glycine is converted to serine.

2.4

Name the three organelles involved in the photorespiratory pathway.

Answer

Chloroplast, peroxisome and mitochondrion.

2.5

State whether each change increases or decreases the rate of photorespiration relative to carboxylation in a C3 leaf.

  1. Higher leaf temperature
  2. Stomatal closure during drought
  3. Higher atmospheric CO₂ concentration
Answer
  1. Increases (the solubility of CO₂ falls more than that of O₂, and Rubisco’s specificity for CO₂ decreases).
  2. Increases (the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf falls).
  3. Decreases.

C3, C4 and CAM

3.1

Complete the table.

C3 C4 CAM
Enzyme of initial CO₂ fixation
First stable product

Stomata open mainly
Photorespiration
Answer
C3 C4 CAM
Enzyme of initial CO₂ fixation Rubisco PEP carboxylase PEP carboxylase
First stable product 3-phosphoglycerate (3 C) Oxaloacetate (4 C) Oxaloacetate (4 C), stored as malate
Separation of initial fixation and Calvin cycle None Spatial: mesophyll and bundle-sheath cells Temporal: night and day

In all three, the CO₂ is finally fixed by Rubisco in the Calvin–Benson cycle.

3.2

Classify each plant as C3, C4 or CAM: maize, wheat, pineapple, sugarcane, rice, prickly pear cactus (Opuntia).

Answer

C3: wheat, rice. C4: maize, sugarcane. CAM: pineapple, Opuntia.

3.3

Which observation is characteristic of a CAM plant?

  1. Bundle-sheath cells packed with chloroplasts
  2. Acidity of the leaf tissue rises during the night and falls during the day
  3. Stomata open only at midday
  4. Rubisco is absent
Answer
  1. Malic acid accumulates in the vacuole at night and is decarboxylated during the day to supply CO₂ to Rubisco. Option a describes C4 (Kranz) anatomy.

3.4

Name the advantage and the cost of the C4 CO₂-concentrating mechanism.

Answer

Advantage: high CO₂ around Rubisco in the bundle sheath suppresses photorespiration, giving higher photosynthesis and water-use efficiency in warm, bright conditions. Cost: extra ATP (2 ATP per CO₂) to regenerate PEP from pyruvate.

Light-response curves and units

4.1

The net CO₂ exchange of one leaf is measured at four light levels. Positive values are net uptake.

Light (µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹) Net CO₂ exchange (µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹)
0 −2
50 0
200 6
800 9
  1. What is the rate of respiration in darkness?
  2. What is the light compensation point?
  3. Calculate the increase in net CO₂ uptake per additional µmol photons between 50 and 200, and between 200 and 800 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹.
  4. Assuming respiration is unchanged in light, what is gross photosynthesis at 200 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹?
Answer
  1. 2 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹.
  2. 50 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹, where net exchange is zero.
  3. 6 / 150 = 0.04 µmol CO₂ per µmol photons; 3 / 600 = 0.005 µmol CO₂ per µmol photons. The response saturates at high light.
  4. 6 + 2 = 8 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹.

4.2

A leaf of area 0.02 m² takes up CO₂ at a constant net rate of 6 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ for 10 min.

  1. Calculate the total net CO₂ uptake in µmol.
  2. Convert it to mg CO₂ (M(CO₂) = 44 g mol⁻¹).
Answer
  1. 6 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ × 0.02 m² × 600 s = 72 µmol CO₂.
  2. 72 × 10⁻⁶ mol × 44 g mol⁻¹ = 3.2 × 10⁻³ g = 3.2 mg CO₂.

Sources, sinks and phloem transport

5.1

Classify each organ as a source or a sink.

  1. Mature, illuminated leaf
  2. Developing fruit
  3. Root tip
  4. Young, expanding leaf
  5. Potato tuber sprouting in spring
Answer
  1. Source. b. Sink. c. Sink. d. Sink. e. Source (stored starch is mobilised to supply the new shoot).

5.2

Put the steps of the pressure-flow model of phloem transport in order:

  1. hydrostatic pressure rises in the sieve tubes at the source
  2. sucrose is loaded into the sieve-element–companion-cell complex
  3. sucrose is unloaded at the sink and water leaves the sieve tubes
  4. water enters the sieve tubes from the xylem by osmosis
  5. sap moves by bulk flow towards the sink
Answer

b → d → a → e → c.

5.3

Name the main sugar transported in the phloem of most plants and the main storage carbohydrate in chloroplasts.

Answer

Sucrose is transported in the phloem; starch is stored in chloroplasts.

Respiration and nitrogen assimilation

6.1

Complete the table for aerobic respiration.

Electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation
Answer

6.2

Roots of a flooded plant switch to fermentation.

  1. Why can glycolysis continue during fermentation?
  2. Name the main fermentation products in plant roots.
  3. About how many ATP are formed per glucose by fermentation, compared with complete aerobic oxidation?
Answer
  1. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH, which glycolysis requires.
  2. Ethanol and CO₂ (and some lactate).
  3. 2 ATP per glucose by fermentation, compared with about 30 ATP by aerobic respiration.

6.3

Put the steps of nitrate assimilation in order and name the enzyme for each step: ammonium → glutamine; nitrite → ammonium; nitrate → nitrite; glutamine + 2-oxoglutarate → glutamate.

Answer
  1. Nitrate → nitrite: nitrate reductase (cytosol).
  2. Nitrite → ammonium: nitrite reductase (plastid).
  3. Ammonium + glutamate → glutamine: glutamine synthetase (GS).
  4. Glutamine + 2-oxoglutarate → 2 glutamate: glutamate synthase (GOGAT).

The reducing power, ATP and carbon skeletons (2-oxoglutarate) come from photosynthesis and respiration, so nitrogen assimilation depends on carbon metabolism.

Explore: Light-response curve

The demo plots net CO₂ uptake of one sun leaf against light, for a C3 leaf (wheat) or a C4 leaf (maize). Use the Light slider (or drag the round handle) to read the curve; the table gives net and gross photosynthesis, dark respiration, the CO₂ released by photorespiration, the light compensation point and what limits the rate. The scenario buttons (“Hot, dry”, “1000 ppm”, “Cool, 12 °C”) change CO₂, temperature and stomata but keep the light where it is. Press Reset before each question.

E1.1

Start values: C3 leaf, 420 ppm CO₂, 25 °C, light 600 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹.

  1. Read off net uptake, gross photosynthesis and dark respiration. Show that the three numbers are consistent.
  2. What limits the rate at this light: light or Rubisco?
  3. Move the light to 0. What is the net uptake now, and why is it negative?
Answer
  1. Net 14.7, gross 15.9 and respiration 1.2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Net = gross − respiration: 15.9 − 1.2 = 14.7.
  2. Light. At 2000 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ the limit switches to Rubisco (net about 16), where the curve flattens.
  3. −1.2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. In darkness there is no photosynthesis, but mitochondrial respiration continues and releases CO₂.

E1.2

Tick “Zoom in on low light”. Read off the light compensation point of the C3 leaf at 25 °C, then press “C4 leaf (maize)” and read it again. Which leaf has the lower compensation point, and by about how much?

Answer

C3: 25 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. C4: 23 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. The two are almost the same: at 25 °C both leaves have the same dark respiration (1.2), and at low light the C4 leaf’s extra ATP cost per CO₂ roughly cancels the C3 leaf’s loss to photorespiration.

E1.3

Reset (C3, light 600) and press “Hot, dry” (38 °C, stomata nearly closed).

  1. Read off net uptake, the CO₂ released by photorespiration (with the number of O₂ fixed per CO₂) and dark respiration. Compare with E1.1.
  2. Now press “C4 leaf (maize)” and “Hot, dry” again. What is the net uptake of the C4 leaf?
  3. Which enzyme explains the difference between b and a?
Answer
  1. Net uptake falls from 14.7 to 1.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Photorespiration rises from 2.7 to 5.4 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (O₂/CO₂ from 0.29 to 1.10), and respiration rises from 1.2 to 3.0. Closed stomata lower the CO₂ inside the leaf, and heat raises Rubisco’s oxygenation relative to carboxylation.
  2. 13.8 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, about nine times the C3 leaf.
  3. PEP carboxylase. It does not react with O₂ and binds CO₂ (as HCO₃⁻) at low concentration, so the C4 leaf concentrates CO₂ around Rubisco in the bundle sheath and photorespiration stays near zero (0.3).

E1.4

Reset and press “1000 ppm” (a greenhouse with CO₂ enrichment), light 600.

  1. Read off net uptake and photorespiration of the C3 leaf.
  2. Press “C4 leaf (maize)”. Does raising CO₂ from 420 to 1000 ppm increase the net uptake of the C4 leaf at this light?
Answer
  1. Net 18.9 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (up from 14.7); photorespiration 1.3 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (down from 2.7; O₂/CO₂ 0.12). More CO₂ wins the competition with O₂ at Rubisco’s active site.
  2. No: it stays at 29.7 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, because the C4 leaf already concentrates CO₂ and is light-limited at 600. This is why CO₂ enrichment benefits C3 crops more than C4 crops.

E1.5

Reset and press “Cool, 12 °C” (light 600). Read off net uptake for the C3 leaf, then press “C4 leaf (maize)” and “Cool, 12 °C”. Which leaf takes up more CO₂, and what limits the C4 leaf?

Answer

C3: 10.1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. C4: 6.0 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The C3 leaf wins. The C4 leaf is limited by enzyme capacity: its enzymes are cold-sensitive, and it pays 2 extra ATP per CO₂, which is not worth it when photorespiration is already low in the cold (C3 O₂/CO₂ is only 0.15 at 12 °C). This is why C4 grasses dominate warm regions and C3 grasses cool ones.

R1. Net and gross photosynthesis

Respiration is estimated as the CO₂ released in darkness, and gross uptake = net uptake + respiration. Run the code. What is gross uptake at 600 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹, and between which two light levels does net uptake rise most steeply per photon?

light <- c(0, 50, 200, 600, 1200)   # umol photons m^-2 s^-1
net   <- c(-2, 0, 5, 10, 11)        # umol CO2 m^-2 s^-1
resp  <- -net[1]                    # CO2 released in darkness
gross <- net + resp
gross
diff(net) / diff(light)             # slope between neighbouring points
Answer 
gross
## [1]  0  2  7 12 13
diff(net) / diff(light)
## [1] 0.040000000 0.033333333 0.012500000 0.001666667

Gross uptake at 600 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ is 12 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹. The slope is steepest between 0 and 50 (0.040) and falls with each step, as the curve saturates.

R3. Light compensation point by linear interpolation

For plant L02, net uptake changes sign between 50 and 200 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Draw a straight line between those two points and find where it crosses zero. Complete y2, run the code, and give the compensation point. Does R’s approx() give the same value (yes/no)?

light <- c(0, 50, 200, 600, 1200)
net   <- c(-2.2, -0.2, 4.6, 9.5, 10.5)   # plant L02
x1 <- 50;  y1 <- -0.2
x2 <- 200; y2 <- ___                      # complete
comp <- x1 + (0 - y1) * (x2 - x1) / (y2 - y1)
comp
approx(x = net, y = light, xout = 0)$y
Answer 
y2 <- 4.6
comp
## [1] 56.25
approx(x = net, y = light, xout = 0)$y

The compensation point is 56.25 (about 56) µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹, and yes, approx() gives the same value.

R4. Water-use efficiency of C3, C4 and CAM leaves

Water-use efficiency (WUE) is CO₂ taken up per water lost. The values below are invented teaching numbers, each measured while the leaf’s stomata are open (the CAM leaf at night). Run the code. Give the WUE of each leaf (µmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O) and which leaf has the highest.

leaf <- c("C3 wheat", "C4 maize", "CAM pineapple")
A    <- c(12, 18, 4)        # CO2 uptake, umol m^-2 s^-1
E    <- c(4.0, 3.0, 0.5)    # transpiration, mmol H2O m^-2 s^-1
wue  <- A / E
wue
leaf[which.max(wue)]
Answer 
wue
## [1] 3 6 8
leaf[which.max(wue)]
## [1] "CAM pineapple"

C3: 3, C4: 6, CAM: 8 µmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O. The CAM leaf has the highest WUE because it opens its stomata in cool night air; the C4 leaf beats the C3 leaf because PEP carboxylase lets it fix CO₂ with less open stomata.
